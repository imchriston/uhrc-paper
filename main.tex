\begin{abstract}
The autonomous control of nonlinear systems, such as quadrotor UAVs, has traditionally required the explicit separation of slow outer-loop trajectory planning from fast inner-loop attitude stabilization.
End-to-end learning approaches seek to collapse this hierarchy, but suffer from severe sample inefficiency and compounding covariate shift, which lead to divergence in dynamic environments.
To address these limitations, this work develops the Unified Hierarchical Reasoning Controller (UHRC), a novel cascaded architecture which pairs a two-level recurrent transformer for high-level kinematic guidance with a classical inner-loop Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) attitude regulator.
The UHRC is trained to generate kinematically feasible attitude intent $[\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}, \theta_{\mathrm{ref}}]$.
This formulation inherently respects the multiscale temporal dynamics of the plant, confining the effects of neural prediction error to the slower translational timescale, where the inner PID supplies a stabilizing correction.
The resulting UHRC-PID is evaluated against a classical Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM)-augmented cascaded PID baseline in five structured test cases of increasing geometric complexity which includes open-field transit, symmetric corridor navigation, gap threading, dynamic obstacle avoidance and parametric robustness under simultaneous plant uncertainty.
The hybrid achieves success rates of 20--30\% in the constrained navigation scenarios and 20\% under dynamic obstacle conditions, where both the pure neural policy and the classical SLAM controller record 0\%.
A formal cascade Input-to-State Stability (ISS) guarantee is also established for the rotational subsystem.
These results establish the UHRC-PID as a robust and computationally tractable framework which resolves the divergence modes of flat end-to-end policies, while retaining the reactive spatial reasoning capabilities of learned hierarchical control.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The autonomous control of complex nonlinear systems remains one of the most consequential open problems in control engineering \parencite{iqbal_nonlinear_2017, liu_new_2015, daoutidis_future_2023}.
Systems such as Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), Autonomous Ground Vehicles (UGVs) and high Degree-of-Freedom (DOF) robotic manipulators are characterized by high-dimensional state spaces, nonlinear inter-state couplings, actuator saturation and significant parametric uncertainty \parencite{sadati_two-level_2015, ameli_robust_2021, iqbal_nonlinear_2017, slotine_applied_1991}.
The fundamental difficulties are that the dynamical regimes associated with long-horizon navigation and fast stabilization operate on different timescales.
These dynamical regimes also differ in their sensitivity to model error and impose competing demands on the same system \parencite{liang_nonlinear_2018, abdelmaksoud_control_2020}.
Designing a controller that simultaneously addresses long-horizon planning and fast reactive stabilization across these coupled dynamics is fundamentally a multiscale reasoning problem.
To date, no single architecture has proven universally adequate in satisfying the competing requirements of stability, adaptability and computational tractability \parencite{rojas_complex_2025, goodwin_control_2001}.
 
Classical hierarchical control has long addressed these multiscale requirements by decomposing the control task into distinct levels.
In such a formulation, a high-level supervisory layer generates strategic plans and setpoints, while a low-level regulatory layer carries out fast stabilization and trajectory tracking \parencite{singh_systems_1978, sadati_two-level_2015, scattolini_hierarchical_2007, mesarovic_two_1970, wilson_foundations_1979}.
The theoretical basis for this separation traces to the multilevel systems framework of \textcite{mesarovic_two_1970} and the hierarchical decomposition methods of \textcite{singh_systems_1978} and \textcite{siljak_decentralized_2013}.
These works showed that large-scale optimization problems can be made tractable through their decomposition into coordinated subproblems across levels.
Owing to its established stability theory, modular implementation and interpretable failure modes, hierarchical control has achieved sustained success in aerospace, process control and robotics \parencite{sanders_hierarchical_1998, wang_hierarchical_2024, liu_generalized_2016, bahnasawi_decentralised_1990, roberts_optimal_2001}.
The quadrotor UAV directly exemplifies this paradigm.
Here, the natural timescale separation between slow translational and fast rotational dynamics motivates a cascaded control structure, where an outer-loop position planner generates attitude references for an inner-loop attitude regulator \parencite{hoffmann_quadrotor_2007, liang_nonlinear_2018, bouabdallah_pid_2004, nagaty_control_2013, abdelhay_modeling_2019}.
Model Predictive Control (MPC) provides a systematic means of implementing the supervisory layer, permitting constraint-aware planning with formal stability guarantees at the coordination level \parencite{scattolini_hierarchical_2007, wang_hierarchical_2024, torrente_data-driven_2021}.
 
However, the fixed interface between these layers introduces a structural information bottleneck whose effects become consequential as task complexity increases \parencite{kolchinsky_nonlinear_2019, li_hierarchical_2023, abdelmaksoud_control_2020}.
The high-level planner typically communicates a heavily compressed representation, ordinarily a sequence of kinematic waypoints, to the low-level regulator.
Consequently, information about obstacle proximity, actuator feasibility and local geometric constraints, which may be necessary for downstream control quality, is discarded \parencite{zhu_hierarchical_2024, zhou_two-level_2017, liu_adaptive_2013}.
Conversely, the low-level regulator tracks these references without access to the global context from which they were derived, such that its response to local disturbances is globally suboptimal \parencite{nguyen_identifying_2021, daoutidis_decomposition_2019}.
Fixed rule-based supervisory frameworks also exhibit brittleness when operating in scenarios that were not considered during their design \parencite{sadati_hierarchical_2008, dave_design_2023}.
These limitations are structural, rather than incidental.
They arise from the requirement that the planner and regulator communicate through a prespecified, fixed-dimensional interface, whose information content is chosen at design time and does not adapt online to the requirements of the task \parencite{kolchinsky_nonlinear_2019, zhang_multi-level_2015}.
 
Learning-based approaches have emerged as a principled alternative for addressing these limitations.
End-to-end Reinforcement Learning (RL) permits the joint optimization of planning and regulation without manually specifying their interface \parencite{almahamid_reinforcement_2021, levine_end--end_2016, kiran_deep_2022, shakya_reinforcement_2023}.
Behavioral cloning instead learns directly from expert demonstrations, providing a supervised approach that avoids the credit assignment difficulties associated with RL.
Behavioral cloning has seen substantial success in autonomous driving and manipulation \parencite{levine_end--end_2016, le_mero_survey_2022, ly_learning_2021, block_is_2024}.
Flat end-to-end policies, however, are notoriously sample inefficient and sensitive to reward shaping.
They do not inherently preserve the multiscale temporal structure required for long-horizon UAV navigation, and employ the same computational depth for strategic deliberation and reactive stabilization \parencite{almahamid_reinforcement_2021, agyei_deep_2025}.
Deep RL methods applied directly to quadrotor control have achieved high agility in simulation \parencite{trad_real-time_2024, saviolo_learning_2023}.
Despite this, the sim-to-real gap, sample complexity and lack of formal stability guarantees remain unresolved \parencite{dai_lyapunov-stable_2021, agyei_deep_2025}.
Hybrid neurosymbolic and fuzzy-integrated controllers partially address this brittleness through the combination of learned components and structured decomposition \parencite{tang_fuzzy_2024, mahmoud_fractional-order_2024, tran_hybrid_2021, baheri_hierarchical_2025}.
The supervisory-regulatory interface and its dimensionality nevertheless remain manually specified.
That is, the boundary between planning and regulation is fixed by the designer, rather than learned from data \parencite{borri_design_2019, yu_survey_2023}.
 
Recent developments in neural reasoning architectures offer a substantially different means of addressing this problem.
Test-time scaling methods which repeat computations in latent space \parencite{geiping_scaling_2025, li_seek_2025}, together with recurrent architectures which adapt computational depth to task difficulty \parencite{geiping_scaling_2025, li_system_2025}, suggest that the processing depth required for a decision can be made emergent, rather than fixed.
The Hierarchical Reasoning Model (HRM) of \textcite{wang_hierarchical_2025} comprises two recurrent levels: a high-level module for slow, abstract planning and a low-level module for rapid, detailed computation.
The modules are coupled through a shared carry state, allowing computational depth to be allocated adaptively.
With as few as 1{,}000 training examples, and without pre-training or chain-of-thought supervision, the HRM achieves near-perfect accuracy on complex Sudoku problems and optimal pathfinding in large mazes, for which state-of-the-art Large Language Model (LLM) approaches fail entirely \parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025, huang_towards_2023, feng_efficient_2025}.
The hierarchical role specialization of the two modules emerges during training, rather than being prescribed by the architecture.
This mirrors the hierarchy of representational dimensionality observed across cortical regions of the mammalian brain , where higher-order areas exhibit longer intrinsic timescales and higher-dimensional representations than primary sensory regions \parencite{badre_frontal_2018, huntenburg_large-scale_2018, murray_hierarchy_2014}.
Building on this, \textcite{jin_decoupled_2025} showed that separating planning from execution within a hierarchical framework improves multistep reasoning, further motivating the use of this principle in closed-loop control.
Similarly, the hierarchical neuro-symbolic decision transformer of \textcite{baheri_hierarchical_2025} demonstrates that coupling a symbolic planner with a learned low-level policy through a structured interface outperforms both purely symbolic and purely neural baselines in sequential decision tasks.
This result provides further evidence that separating planning and execution offers a genuine computational advantage beyond that of either component alone.
 
Despite these developments, all demonstrated applications of the HRM have so far been restricted to discrete, turn-based domains.
Its use as a closed-loop controller for continuous, real-time nonlinear dynamical systems remains entirely unexplored in the literature.
In this setting, the control outputs must be physically continuous, observations are supplied as dense sensory streams, and temporal context must be retained over thousands of control cycles.
The extension of HRMs to continuous control requires nontrivial architectural modifications.
Continuous commands must replace discrete token outputs, while the supervised training signal must be obtained from a physically meaningful expert policy instead of symbolic ground truth labels.
Also, localization and mapping, which are prerequisites for meaningful planning, must be supplied by an onboard sensor, as supported by \cite{gupta_simultaneous_2022, milijas_comparison_2021, munguia_uav_2025, chen_slam_2022}, with its outputs incorporated into the continuous observation stream.
 
To address this gap, we propose and evaluate the \textit{Unified Hierarchical Reasoning Controller} (UHRC) within a cascaded hybrid architecture for autonomous navigation of a six-degree-of-freedom quadrotor UAV.
This work constitutes the first systematic application of the HRM architecture to a continuous nonlinear closed-loop control system.
The UHRC adapts the two-level reasoning structure of the HRM into a recurrent transformer, which maps a 45-dimensional body-frame observation to desired attitude references $[\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}, \theta_{\mathrm{ref}}]$.
The observation contains the kinematic state, goal displacement and a 32-ray planar Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) scan.
Behavioral cloning, employed by\cite{levine_end--end_2016, le_mero_survey_2022, block_is_2024} is used to train the UHRC on expert trajectories generated by an A* path planner over 500 simulated episodes \parencite{lavalle_planning_2006, qin_review_2023}.
A classical cascaded Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) attitude regulator tracks the resulting references, providing formal inner-loop stability and integral disturbance rejection.
In this implementation, the proposed architecture is compared with a classical hierarchical baseline comprising Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM)-based localization \parencite{gupta_simultaneous_2022, milijas_comparison_2021}, A* path planning and cascaded PID control \parencite{bouabdallah_pid_2004, ren_cascade_2016, abdelhay_modeling_2019, lopez-sanchez_pid_2023}.
The baseline's regulation layer is chosen as cascaded PID for several reasons.
PID remains the dominant regulation law on
embedded flight controllers (PX4, ArduPilot, and the Crazyflie's own onboard firmware),
making it the more deployment relevant comparator for a system explicitly targeting
resource-constrained hardware, where MPC's online optimisation is frequently
infeasible at the required control rate. Further motivation is that the PID is the dominant
controller in industrial practice more broadly \parencite{astrom_pid_1995, astrom_future_2001}.
Making it the most practically representative classical baseline available. 
The comparison considers five structured test cases of increasing environmental complexity: open-field transit (TC01), symmetric corridor navigation (TC02), gap threading (TC03), dynamic obstacle avoidance (TC04) and parametric robustness under simultaneous plant model uncertainty (TC05).

Owing to the absence of HRM-based control methods for continuous dynamical systems, the main contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The first empirical demonstration of an HRM-based closed-loop controller for a continuous nonlinear dynamic system.
          This also constitutes the first use of the architecture in a hybrid cascade with a classical inner-loop regulator.

    \item A hybrid UHRC-PID architecture which generates bounded, smooth attitude references through a neural outer loop, for tracking by a PD attitude regulator.
          This separation decouples spatial reasoning from low-level stabilization and resolves the instability caused by compounding errors in flat end-to-end behavioral cloning.

    \item A formal stability analysis establishing Local Exponential Stability (LES) of the inner loop through Lyapunov synthesis, Input-to-State Stability (ISS) of the forced rotational subsystem and cascade Uniform Ultimate Boundedness (UUB) of the rotational state.
          The alignment condition necessary for goal convergence is also identified.

    \item A rigorous comparative empirical evaluation of the performance-robustness trade-offs across all five test cases.
          The evaluation shows a 20\% success rate under dynamic obstacle conditions, where both the pure neural policy and the classical SLAM controller fail, together with 20--30\% success in constrained geometric environments.
\end{enumerate}

The proposed UHRC-PID architecture, observation representation, training procedure and classical baseline are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:method}.
The formal cascade stability analysis is then presented in Section~\ref{sec:stability}.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the quantitative results for the five test cases, followed by a discussion of the performance-robustness trade-off and its architectural origins in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Quadrotor Dynamics Model}
\label{sec:dynamics}

The benchmark system is a rigid-body quadrotor modelled with respect to the East-North-Up (ENU) inertial frame $\mathcal{I}$ and a body frame $\mathcal{B}$ fixed at the vehicle's center of mass.
Its state vector is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x} = \bigl[\mathbf{r}_I^{\top},\;
                        \mathbf{v}_I^{\top},\;
                        \mathbf{q}_{BI}^{\top},\;
                        \boldsymbol{\omega}_B^{\top},\;
                        \boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top} \bigr]^{\top}
    \in \mathbb{R}^{17},
    \label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{r}_I \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and $\mathbf{v}_I \in \mathbb{R}^3$ denote the inertial position and velocity, respectively.
The unit quaternion $\mathbf{q}_{BI} \in \mathbb{R}^4$ represents the rotation from $\mathcal{I}$ to $\mathcal{B}$ and satisfies $\|\mathbf{q}_{BI}\| = 1$, while $\boldsymbol{\omega}_B \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the angular velocity expressed in $\mathcal{B}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Omega} \in \mathbb{R}^4$ contains the four rotor speeds.
The rotor speeds are included solely for measurement.

We take the control input as the body-frame wrench
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{u} = \bigl[T,\; \tau_x,\; \tau_y,\; \tau_z\bigr]^{\top}
    \in \mathbb{R}^4,
\end{equation}
which consists of the net collective thrust $T$ along the body $+z$ axis and the roll, pitch and yaw torques $\tau_x$, $\tau_y$ and $\tau_z$, respectively.
The formulation assumes ideal wrench actuation, bypassing the explicit rotor speed dynamics, as is appropriate for the simulation study considered here \parencite{hoffmann_quadrotor_2007}.

Applying Newton's second law gives the inertial-frame translational dynamics as
\begin{equation}
    m\dot{\mathbf{v}}_I =
        mg +
        R_{IB}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ T\end{bmatrix} +
        C_d^{\text{lin}}\,\mathbf{v}_I,
    \label{eq:trans}
\end{equation}
where $m$ denotes the vehicle mass and $g$ the gravitational acceleration.
The matrix $R_{IB} = R_{BI}^{\top}$ describes the rotation from $\mathcal{B}$ to $\mathcal{I}$, obtained from $\mathbf{q}_{BI}$, and $C_d^{\text{lin}} = \mathrm{diag}(c_x, c_y, c_z)$ is the linear aerodynamic drag matrix.
The body-frame rotational dynamics are described by the Newton-Euler equations,
\begin{equation}
    J\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_B =
        \boldsymbol{\tau}_B -
        \boldsymbol{\omega}_B \times J\boldsymbol{\omega}_B -
        C_d^{\text{rot}}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_B,
    \label{eq:rot}
\end{equation}
where $J = \mathrm{diag}(I_{xx}, I_{yy}, I_{zz})$ is the diagonal inertia matrix, $\boldsymbol{\tau}_B = [\tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z]^{\top}$, and $C_d^{\text{rot}} = \mathrm{diag}(d_x, d_y, d_z)$ is the rotational damping matrix.
The attitude evolves according to the unit quaternion differential equation,
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\mathbf{q}}_{BI} =
        \tfrac{1}{2}\,(\mathbf{q}_{BI})\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_B,
\end{equation}
where $(\mathbf{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times3}$ denotes the standard quaternion kinematic matrix \parencite{shastry_development_2018}.
A fourth-order Runge-Kutta scheme is used to integrate the system at a fixed timestep of $\Delta t = 0.01$\,s, with the quaternion renormalized after each step.

\subsection{UHRC Architecture}
\label{sec:uhrc_arch}

The UHRC-PID is a two-level recurrent transformer, which adapts the HRM architecture proposed by \textcite{wang_hierarchical_2025} to continuous closed-loop control.
Planning and stabilization are decoupled and assigned to the UHRC and PID, respectively.
At each timestep, the coupled UHRC modules produce a 2-dimensional attitude reference which expresses the strategic intent.
These subgoals are supplied to the lower-level PID to generate the required torque wrench.
The UHRC modules retain their memory in a rolling carry buffer, replacing the per-problem hidden state of the HRM.
This permits temporal reasoning throughout the episode horizon.

The 45-dimensional body-frame observation $\mathbf{o}$ is mapped by the UHRC to the commanded attitude intent vector
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ref}} = [\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}, \theta_{\mathrm{ref}}]^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^2.
\end{equation}
The yaw reference is excluded ($\psi_{\mathrm{ref}} = 0$), since the body-frame observations are heading-invariant and the expert commands zero yaw throughout transit.
The hover thrust bias is also initialized to $0.0$, as the model predicts angular deviations directly, rather than thrust forces.

The resulting attitude intent is supplied to a classical inner-loop PID regulator.
This regulator computes the body wrench $\mathbf{u} = [T, \tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z]^{\top}$ required to track the commanded roll and pitch, while also regulating altitude and yaw:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_i(t) = K_{p,i} e_i(t) + K_{i,i} \int_{0}^{t} e_i(\tau) d\tau + K_{d,i} \frac{d e_i(t)}{dt}, \quad i \in \{\phi, \theta\}
\end{equation}
where $e_\phi(t) = \phi_{\mathrm{ref}}(t) - \phi(t)$ and $e_\theta(t) = \theta_{\mathrm{ref}}(t) - \theta(t)$. 

Restricting the neural network to the outer loop removes the high-frequency stabilization task from the UHRC.
The inner-loop PID then converts the generated spatial trajectories into dynamically feasible actuator commands.
Thus, the architecture guarantees local asymptotic stability of the attitude dynamics while preserving the multiscale spatial reasoning capabilities of the HRM.


\subsubsection{Observation Representation}
\label{sec:obs}

The observations are expressed entirely in the body frame $\mathcal{B}$.
This representation is invariant to global heading and position, reducing the domain of the mapping to be learned.
That is, the same avoidance maneuver applies independently of the vehicle's absolute orientation in the world frame \parencite{liang_nonlinear_2018}.
We define the 45-dimensional observation vector as

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{o} =
    \bigl[
        \underbrace{\mathbf{v}_B^{\top}}_{\times 3},\;
        \underbrace{\boldsymbol{\omega}_B^{\top}}_{\times 3},\;
        \underbrace{\mathbf{g}_B^{\top}}_{\times 3},\;
        \underbrace{\Delta\mathbf{r}_B^{\top}}_{\times 3},\;
        \underbrace{d_{\mathrm{norm}}}_{\times 1},\;
        \underbrace{\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{lidar}}^{\top}}_{\times 32}
    \bigr]^{\top}
    \in \mathbb{R}^{45},
    \label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}_B = R_{BI}\mathbf, {v}_I$ denotes the body-frame translational velocity, $\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$ the angular velocity and $\mathbf{g}_B = R_{BI}[0, 0, -g]^{\top}$ the gravity vector.
The goal displacement $\Delta\mathbf{r}_B = R_{BI}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{goal}} - \mathbf{r}_I)$ is clipped to a maximum range of $r_{\max}^{\mathrm{goal}} = 20$\,m, and $d_{\mathrm{norm}} = \min(\|\Delta\mathbf{r}_B\|, 20) / 20$ is the normalized goal distance.
The term $\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{lidar}} \in [0, 1]^{32}$ denotes a 32-ray planar LiDAR scan, with field of view $\pi$\,rad and maximum range 5.0\,m, normalized to the unit interval.

The scalar $d_{\mathrm{norm}}$ is included separately to retain goal-distance information.
Without this term, the goal displacement vector $\Delta\mathbf{r}_B$ loses magnitude information as the drone approaches the goal, resulting in overshoot.
Before being supplied to the model at runtime, the observation is standardized using precomputed dataset statistics.


\subsubsection{Dual-Stream Input Encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}

Separate encoder branches process the 13 scalar kinematic features $[\mathbf{v}_B^{\top}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_B^{\top}, \mathbf{g}_B^{\top}, \Delta\mathbf{r}_B^{\top}, d_{\mathrm{norm}}]$ and the 32-ray LiDAR scan before their outputs are fused.

The 13-dimensional input is first projected to $\mathbb{R}^{h/2}$ by a two-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), using the scaled hyperbolic tangent
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{s} = W_2 \tanh\!\bigl(\sqrt{H}\cdot W_1 \mathbf{o}_{\mathrm{kin}} + \mathbf{b}_1\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $h = 128$ denotes the model's hidden dimension.
The scaling factor $\sqrt{H}$ expands the effective dynamic range of the kinematic inputs into the region where $\tanh$ supplies informative gradients, thereby preventing early saturation during training.

A two-stage convolutional encoder processes the 32 normalized range returns as a 1D signal,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{l} = W_{\mathrm{proj}}\,
        \mathrm{Flatten}\!\bigl(
            \sigma\!\bigl(\mathrm{Conv}_{k=3}^{16}(
                \sigma(\mathrm{Conv}_{k=5}^{16}(\mathbf{o}_{\mathrm{lidar}})))\bigr)
        \bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the Sigmoid Linear Unit (SiLU) activation and $\mathrm{Conv}_{k}^{c}$ denotes a 1D convolution with $c$ output channels, kernel size $k$ and same padding.
The projection $W_{\mathrm{proj}} \in \mathbb{R}^{(h/2) \times 512}$ maps the flattened $16 \times 32 = 512$ feature map to $\mathbb{R}^{h/2}$.

The two $h/2$-dimensional branch outputs are concatenated and linearly projected to the single embedding $\mathbf{e}_t = W_f[\mathbf{s}; \mathbf{l}] \in \mathbb{R}^{h}$, representing the observation at timestep $t$.


\subsubsection{Transformer Reasoning Blocks}
\label{sec:blocks}

The $H$- and $L$-modules each comprise stacked \texttt{UHRCBlock} layers, which implement the standard pre-normalized transformer encoder block
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{z}' &= \mathrm{RMSNorm}\bigl(\mathbf{z} + \mathrm{MHA}(\mathbf{z})\bigr),
    \label{eq:attn_block} \\
    \mathbf{z}'' &= \mathrm{RMSNorm}\bigl(\mathbf{z}' + \mathrm{SwiGLU}(\mathbf{z}')\bigr),
    \label{eq:mlp_block}
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{MHA}(\cdot)$ denotes non-causal multi-head self-attention with $n_h = 4$ heads and head dimension $d_h = h / n_h = 32$.
The gated feed-forward network $\mathrm{SwiGLU}(\cdot)$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SwiGLU}(\mathbf{z}) =
        W_{\downarrow}\bigl(\mathrm{SiLU}(W_g \mathbf{z}) \odot W_u \mathbf{z}\bigr),
\end{equation}
with intermediate dimension $d_{\mathrm{ff}} = 512$.
Since the carry contains a temporal history, rather than an autoregressive prediction sequence, non-causal attention is appropriate.
Each position may therefore attend to every other position, including those corresponding to more recent observations.

Rotary Positional Encoding (RoPE) is applied separately to the query and key matrices of each attention head to encode the relative age of positions in the carry buffer.
For sequence length $T$ and head dimension $d_h$, the rotary frequencies are defined as
\begin{equation}
    \theta_i = \frac{1}{10000^{2i/d_h}}, \quad i = 0, 1, \ldots, d_h/2 - 1,
\end{equation}
RoPE is used in place of learned absolute positional embeddings because it generalizes to sequence positions outside those encountered during training \parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025}.


\subsubsection{Persistent Temporal Carry Buffer}
\label{sec:carry}

The original HRM re-initializes the hidden states $\mathbf{z}_H$ and $\mathbf{z}_L$ for each input problem.
Closed-loop control, however, requires the retention of information across successive timesteps to track velocity history, recognize developing obstacle geometries and anticipate the evolution of the trajectory.
Accordingly, the UHRC represents its hidden states as persistent rolling buffers,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_{H}, \mathbf{z}_{L} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times h},
\end{equation}
where $B$ denotes the batch size (1 at inference) and $C = 16$ the carry length.
At each timestep, the observation embedding $\mathbf{e}_t$ is appended and the oldest buffer entry is removed:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{roll\_carry}(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{e}_t) =
        \bigl[\mathbf{z}_{:,1:C,:};\; \mathbf{e}_t^{\top}\bigr]
    \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times h},
\end{equation}
thereby retaining a fixed-length window of temporal context without unbounded memory growth.


\subsubsection{H-L Mutual Refinement and One-Step Gradient Approximation}
\label{sec:hl_cycles}

The $H$- and $L$-modules are coupled through $H_\mathrm{cyc} = 2$ outer cycles, with $L_\mathrm{cyc} = 2$ inner $L$-module passes per cycle.
At each step, concatenating the carry tokens and current observation embedding gives the full sequence $\mathbf{z}_H^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 17 \times h}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_H^{(t)} = \bigl[\mathbf{Z}_H;\; \mathbf{e}_t^{\top}\bigr], \quad
    \mathbf{z}_L^{(t)} = \bigl[\mathbf{Z}_L;\; \mathbf{e}_t^{\top}\bigr].
\end{equation}
The observation re-injection tensor $\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{obs}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 17 \times h}$ is constructed with zeros at the carry positions and $\mathbf{e}_t$ at position 16.
Thus, the raw observation remains available as an additive bias during every $L$-module call \parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025}.

The one-step gradient approximation used in \textcite{wang_hierarchical_2025} is employed to bound the depth of the gradient graph and preserve training stability.
All module calls preceding the final call in the refinement cycle are evaluated under \texttt{torch.no\_grad()}, such that only a single $L$-pass followed by a single $H$-pass contributes to the gradient.


\subsubsection{Subgoal Generation, Gated Injection, and Attitude Reference Output}
\label{sec:subgoal}

After the final $H$-module update, a two-layer MLP planning head decodes the terminal hidden state $\mathbf{z}_H[:,16,:] \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times h}$, corresponding to the most recent position in the carry sequence, into a 2-dimensional horizontal velocity subgoal in the body frame:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}} = W_2\,\mathrm{SiLU}(W_1 \mathbf{z}_H[:,16,:]) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 2}.
\end{equation}
This subgoal represents the H-module's strategic intent through the desired body-frame velocity vector $[v_x, v_y]$, giving a physically interpretable interface between the modules.
The vertical component $v_z$ is omitted because altitude tracking is effectively decoupled and controlled by the independent altitude regulator.

A gated additive mechanism injects the subgoal into the $L$-module, modulating its reactive representation without overriding it:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{g} &= \sigma\bigl(W_g \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}} + \mathbf{b}_g\bigr) \in (0,1)^{B \times h}, \label{eq:gate} \\
    \mathbf{c} &= \bigl(W_p \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}}\bigr) \odot \mathbf{g} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times h}, \label{eq:context}
\end{align}
where $\sigma$ denotes the element-wise sigmoid.
The gate bias is initialized to $\mathbf{b}_g = +2.0$, giving $\sigma(+2) \approx 0.88$ at the beginning of training.
The gate therefore remains mostly open, permitting the subgoal signal to propagate freely before convergence of the planning head.
The gated context is broadcast over the carry sequence, after which the $L$-module carries out a final two-layer attention pass:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_L^{\mathrm{final}} = \mathcal{L}\bigl(\mathbf{z}_L + \mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{17}^{\top}\bigr),
\end{equation}
The attitude reference action is then decoded from the terminal position:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{u}} = W_a\,\mathbf{z}_L^{\mathrm{final}}[:,16,:] = [\hat{\phi}_{\mathrm{ref}},\; \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}}]^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{2}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Expert Data Generation}
\label{sec:datagen}


The training data comprise 500 successful simulated episodes, generated by an expert policy consisting of an A* path planner, a pure-pursuit tracker and the cascaded PID attitude controller, with $\Delta t = 0.01$\,s per step \parencite{levine_end--end_2016, le_mero_survey_2022}.


At the beginning of each episode, a 2D occupancy grid is formed with resolution $r_{\mathrm{grid}} = 0.25$\,m per cell.
The grid spans the convex hull of the start position, goal position and all obstacle extents, together with a 2.0\,m margin.
Circular obstacles are inflated by $r_{\mathrm{inf}} = 0.25$\,m to account for the vehicle's body radius.
An A* search over an 8-connected neighborhood, using the octile heuristic, guarantees the shortest collision-free path when one exists \parencite{zhou_review_2021}.
Episodes without a feasible path are discarded before simulation.


Three passes of a cubic averaging filter smooth the planned path, which is then tracked by a pure-pursuit controller with lookahead $L_d = 1.0$\,m and maximum speed $V_{\max} = 2.0$\,m/s.
Within 2\,m of the goal, the commanded speed is reduced linearly to $0.3$\,m/s.
Once the vehicle enters the $0.1$\,m goal acceptance radius, a zero velocity command is maintained for a further 150 steps (1.5\,s), demonstrating the hover termination behavior to the model.


To promote generalization of the policy beyond the default $+x$-direction transit, six categories of episodes are sampled using the distribution in Table~\ref{tab:episode_types}.

The omnidirectional episodes are necessary to eliminate the directional bias of the predominantly $+x$-transit training distribution.
In their absence, the learned policy fails to navigate in the $-x$ or $\pm y$ directions.

Every 50 steps, a Gaussian velocity perturbation ($\sigma = 0.2$\,m/s) and a small random yaw offset ($|\delta\psi| \leq 0.15$\,rad) are applied to the current state with probability 0.3.
This Dataset Aggregation (DAgger)-style augmentation, implemented by \textcite{levine_end--end_2016}, broadens the distribution of states encountered by the expert and prevents the compounding errors associated with pure behavioral cloning.


An episode is retained only when (i) the drone reaches the goal without collision, (ii) the goal distance decreases monotonically over coarse 100-step windows and (iii) the mean magnitude of the velocity subgoal in the final hover phase is below $0.3$\,m/s.
The raw A* tracker outputs are post-processed to make the expert signal learnable by the neural policy.
An Exponential Moving Average (EMA) filter smooths high-frequency discontinuities in the expert commands, using smoothing factors $\alpha_{\mathrm{act}} = 0.12$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{sub}} = 0.08$.
A hover deadband override also demonstrates stable termination by clamping $\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to zero upon entry into the 0.1\,m goal acceptance radius.
The attitude commands are strictly limited to $\pm 30^{\circ}$, ensuring that the demonstrations remain within the feasible limits of the low-level PID.

\subsection{Behavioral Cloning Training}
\label{sec:training}


\subsubsection{Dataset Construction}

The \texttt{ControlDataset} forms sequence windows of $T_{\mathrm{seq}} = 8$ timesteps from the stored episodes.
Each window is confined to a single episode, and windows beginning within its first $T_{\mathrm{seq}} - 1$ steps are left-padded with zeros.
The 45-dimensional observations are $z$-scored using the dataset-wide mean and standard deviation, while the attitude references remain in their original physical units.
Training and validation subsets are separated by episode, with 10\% reserved for validation.

\subsubsection{Loss Function}
\label{sec:loss_function}

The total loss is defined as a weighted sum of the action and subgoal tracking errors, scaled by a spatial proximity multiplier:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \gamma_{\mathrm{spat}} \Bigl( \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}} + \alpha_s \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sub}} + \alpha_t \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{trk}} \Bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_s = 0.1$ and $\alpha_t = 0.0$ are the selected loss hyperparameters.
The spatial factor $\gamma_{\mathrm{spat}}$ increases the penalty by $10\times$ when the vehicle is within a normalized distance threshold of the goal.


The action imitation loss is formulated as a weighted Mean Squared Error (MSE) of the attitude vector,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}} = 
        \Bigl\langle 
            \bigl(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{ref}} - \mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ref}}^*\bigr)^{\!\top} 
            \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{w}_a) 
            \bigl(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{ref}} - \mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ref}}^*\bigr) 
        \Bigr\rangle,
\end{equation}
where the normalized action weights are initialized equally to $\mathbf{w}_a = [10.0, 10.0]$.
The intermediate horizontal velocity intent is supervised through the corresponding subgoal loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sub}} = 
        \Bigl\langle 
            \bigl(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}} - \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{sub}}^*\bigr)^{\!\top} 
            \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{w}_s) 
            \bigl(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}} - \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{sub}}^*\bigr) 
        \Bigr\rangle,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{w}_s = [10.0, 10.0]$ gives the corresponding subgoal weights.

\subsection{Comparative Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval_protocol}


The controllers are compared over five structured test cases (TC01--TC05) of increasing environmental complexity.
Both receive ideal state feedback from the same simulation state to ensure a fair comparison.
Each case comprises $N = 30$ randomized Monte Carlo trials, with performance metrics averaged over successful and failed trials.

\textbf{TC01 -- Open Transit.} An obstacle-free, straight-line goal-seeking task establishes the baseline trajectory tracking performance.

\textbf{TC02 -- Symmetric Corridor.} Six obstacles are placed symmetrically about the nominal flight path to evaluate multi-obstacle avoidance and path quality under spatial constraints.

\textbf{TC03 -- Gap Navigation.} A 3\,m gap formed by two obstacles lies directly in the nominal flight path, permitting evaluation of control precision and gap-threading capability.

\textbf{TC04 -- Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance.} Obstacles are withheld from the controller's sensor data until $t \geq 3.0$\,s, when their revelation requires reactive replanning during flight.
Identical sensor data are supplied to both controllers at every timestep.

\textbf{TC05 -- Parametric Robustness.} The obstacle arrangement of TC02 is retained while the plant parameters are perturbed: mass by $\pm 20$\%, diagonal inertia by $\pm 30$\%, drag coefficients by $\pm 50$\% and thrust coefficient $k_T$ by $\pm 15$\%.
This test characterizes each controller's robustness margin under distributional shift.

Performance is evaluated using the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of the Euclidean position error, Integral Absolute Error (IAE), Integral Time-weighted Absolute Error (ITAE), Path Length Ratio (PLR = actual path length / straight-line distance) and episode success rate.
An episode is successful when the vehicle reaches within 0.5\,m of the goal without collision.


\subsection{Stability Analysis}
\label{sec:stability}

\subsubsection{Standing Assumptions}
Let $\boldsymbol{\eta}=[\phi,\theta,\psi]^\top$ denote Euler attitude,
$\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$ body rate, and
$\boldsymbol{\eta}_r=[\phi_{\mathrm{ref}},\theta_{\mathrm{ref}},0]^\top$
the commanded attitude.
\begin{description}
\item[Assumption 1:] The operating domain has
$|\phi|,|\theta|\le\pi/6$, a fixed smooth angle-error branch,
and torque limits $(0.6,0.6,0.35)\,\mathrm{Nm}$. The certificate uses ideal applied body torques.
\item[Assumption 2:] The physical rotational model is
$J\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_B
=\boldsymbol{\tau}-D_\omega\boldsymbol{\omega}_B
-\boldsymbol{\omega}_B\times J\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$
 and standard  Euler kinematics
$\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=\mathcal T(\boldsymbol{\eta})\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$.
The local approximation $\mathcal T(0)=I_3$ defines the nominal
linear model. 
\item[Assumption 3:] Applied roll/pitch references are bounded by
$\pi/6$. Continuous-reference error equations additionally assume
$\boldsymbol{\eta}_r\in W^{2,\infty}$ with specified derivative bounds.

\item[Assumption 4:] Actual thrust satisfies $0\le T(t)\le T_{\max}$,
and horizontal drag satisfies $C_h=C_h^\top\ge c_{\min}I_2$,
$c_{\min}>0$.
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Inner Loop: Attitude Stabilization}
\label{sec:inner_loop}

With $e_\phi=\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}-\phi$, the implemented unsaturated
PD law is $\tau_\phi=K_p e_\phi-K_d p$.
Under $p\simeq\dot\phi$, the nominal local roll-error equation is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 I_{xx}\ddot e_\phi+(K_d+d_x)\dot e_\phi+K_p e_\phi&=d_\phi(t),\\
 d_\phi&=I_{xx}\ddot\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}
 +(K_d+d_x)\dot\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}-\Delta_\phi.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:err_roll}
\end{equation}
Here $d_x$ is rotational damping and $\Delta_\phi$ collects the remaining
terms in the local roll representation. For the exact nonlinear model,
kinematic residuals must be included as well as gyroscopic coupling.
In state form $\mathbf e=[e_\phi,\dot e_\phi]^\top$,
\begin{equation}
 \dot{\mathbf e}=A\mathbf e+Bd_\phi,\quad
 A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-K_p/I_{xx}&-(K_d+d_x)/I_{xx}\end{bmatrix},
 \quad B=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1/I_{xx}\end{bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:err_state}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Nominal Inner-Loop Exponential Stability]
\label{thm:inner}
For $I_{xx}>0$, $A$ is Hurwitz if and only if
$K_p>0$ and $K_d+d_x>0$.
For any $Q=Q^\top>0$, the equation $A^\top P+PA=-Q$ has a unique
$P=P^\top>0$. With $d_\phi=0$,
\begin{equation}
 \|\mathbf e(t)\|\le C\|\mathbf e(0)\|e^{-\alpha t},
 \quad C=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(P)}{\lambda_{\min}(P)}},
 \quad \alpha=\frac{\lambda_{\min}(Q)}{2\lambda_{\max}(P)}.
 \label{eq:les_bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Routh--Hurwitz applied to
$\lambda^2+(K_d+d_x)\lambda/I_{xx}+K_p/I_{xx}$
gives the stated conditions. For $V=\mathbf e^\top P\mathbf e$,
$\dot V=-\mathbf e^\top Q\mathbf e$ and
$\lambda_{\min}(P)\|\mathbf e\|^2\le V
\le\lambda_{\max}(P)\|\mathbf e\|^2$.
Comparison gives \eqref{eq:les_bound}
\parencite{khalil_nonlinear_2002}.
This establishes the nominal result, a nonlinear extension requires
the quantitative conditions stated below.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Nominal Inner-Loop ISS]
\label{cor:iss}
For every admissible fixed pair $(K_p,K_d)$, every $Q>0$, and every
$\kappa\in(0,1)$, the nominal linear system \eqref{eq:err_state} is
globally ISS with respect to its effective input $d_\phi$, with
\begin{equation}
 \gamma_{Q,\kappa}(s)
 =C\,\frac{2\|PB\|}{\kappa\lambda_{\min}(Q)}\,s.
 \label{eq:iss_gain}
\end{equation}
For $D_t=\|d_\phi\|_{\infty,[0,t]}$, a corresponding state estimate is
\begin{equation}
 \|\mathbf e(t)\|\le\max\left\{
 Ce^{-(1-\kappa)\alpha t}\|\mathbf e(0)\|,
 \gamma_{Q,\kappa}(D_t)\right\}.
 \label{eq:iss_state_bound}
\end{equation}
Globality, refers to the linear model.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
On any fixed interval $[0,t]$, let
$q=\lambda_{\min}(Q)$ and $D=D_t$. Then
\[
 \dot V\le-q\|\mathbf e\|^2+2\|PB\|\|\mathbf e\|D.
\]
For every $\kappa\in(0,1)$ and
$\|\mathbf e\|\ge\rho(D)=2\|PB\|D/(\kappa q)$,
$\dot V\le-(1-\kappa)q\|\mathbf e\|^2$.
The sublevel set $V\le\lambda_{\max}(P)\rho(D)^2$ is invariant
on this interval and has outer radius at most $C\rho(D)$.
Above that level,
$\dot V\le-2(1-\kappa)\alpha V$, comparison gives
\eqref{eq:iss_state_bound} and hence \eqref{eq:iss_gain}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Inner-Loop ISS]
\label{prop:sampled_iss}
For sample period $h>0$, let
\[
 A_0=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&-d_x/I_{xx}\end{bmatrix},\quad
 B_0=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1/I_{xx}\end{bmatrix},\quad
 \Phi=e^{A_0h},\quad\Gamma=\int_0^h e^{A_0s}B_0\,ds.
\]
With held torque, set $S=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1)$,
$M=S(\Phi-\Gamma[K_p\ K_d])S$, $E=(1,0)^\top$, and $H=S\Gamma$.
For $y_k=(e_{\phi,k},p_k)^\top$ and
$\delta r_k=\phi_{\mathrm{ref},k+1}-\phi_{\mathrm{ref},k}$,
the nominal update is
$y_{k+1}=My_k+E\delta r_k+Hd_k$,
where $d_k$ is an additive torque held over the interval.
This system is ISS if and only if $M$ is Schur. For real $2\times2$
$M$, this is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
 1-\operatorname{tr}M+\det M>0,\quad
 1+\operatorname{tr}M+\det M>0,\quad
 1-\det M>0.
 \label{eq:sampled_admissible}
\end{equation}
For any $Q_d>0$, let $M^\top P_dM-P_d=-Q_d$ and define
\[
 p_+=\lambda_{\max}(P_d),\quad p_-=\lambda_{\min}(P_d),\quad
 C_d=\sqrt{p_+/p_-},\quad
 \rho_d=\sqrt{1-\lambda_{\min}(Q_d)/p_+}.
\]
Here $p_+$ denotes the maximum and $p_-$ the minimum eigenvalue.
Then
\begin{equation}
 \|y_k\|\le C_d\rho_d^k\|y_0\|+g_rR_k+g_dW_k,\qquad
 g_r=\frac{\|E\|_{P_d}}{\sqrt{p_-}(1-\rho_d)},\quad
 g_d=\frac{\|H\|_{P_d}}{\sqrt{p_-}(1-\rho_d)},
 \label{eq:sampled_bound}
\end{equation}
where $\|v\|_{P_d}=\sqrt{v^\top P_dv}$,
$R_k=\sup_{j<k}|\delta r_j|$, and $W_k=\sup_{j<k}|d_j|$,
with indices $0\le j<k$ and empty suprema zero.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The discrete Lyapunov identity gives
$\|My\|_{P_d}\le\rho_d\|y\|_{P_d}$.
The triangle inequality and the geometric-series bound yield
\eqref{eq:sampled_bound}. The Schur conditions
\eqref{eq:sampled_admissible} are the second-order unit-circle
criterion. General within-interval inputs are handled by bounding their
integrated contribution in the same weighted norm.
\end{proof}

Define the nominal admissible attitude-PID set for axis $i$ by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal K_i(h)=\bigl\{(K_p,K_i,K_d):\;&K_i=0,\ K_p>0,\ K_d+d_i>0,\\
 &1-\operatorname{tr}M_i+\det M_i>0,\\
 &1+\operatorname{tr}M_i+\det M_i>0,\quad1-\det M_i>0\bigr\}.
 \label{eq:pid_admissible_set}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $M_i$ is the held-torque matrix above with the inertia and damping
of axis $i$. Thus $\mathcal K=\mathcal K_\phi\times\mathcal K_\theta
\times\mathcal K_\psi$ satisfies both the continuous Hurwitz and sampled
Schur tests. This is a PD subset of PID parameter space; it makes no
claim for nonzero attitude integral gains. Nonlinear admissibility also
requires the state and reference conditions below.
For a concrete certified subset, let
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal K_{\mathrm{box}}=
 \left\{K\in\mathcal K:
 0.98K_{p,i}^0\le K_{p,i}\le1.02K_{p,i}^0,\quad
 0.98K_{d,i}^0\le K_{d,i}\le1.02K_{d,i}^0\right\},
 \label{eq:pid_box}
\end{equation}
where the nominal attitude gains $K_{p,i}^0,K_{d,i}^0$ are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:gains}. A common certificate below verifies the whole

\subsubsection{Outer Loop: Kinematic Guidance Map}
\label{sec:outer_loop}

Write the applied reference map as
$f_{\mathrm{UHRC}}=\operatorname{sat}_{\pi/6} f_{\mathrm{raw}}$,
mapping $(\mathbf o_k,\mathbf z_k)\in\mathbb R^{45}\times\mathcal Z$
to $\mathcal D=[-\pi/6,\pi/6]^2$, where
$\mathcal Z=\mathbb R^{2\times C\times H}$ is the latent-carry space.

\begin{lemma}[Output Bound]
\label{lem:bounded}
For every finite evaluation,
$f_{\mathrm{UHRC}}(\mathbf o,\mathbf z)\in\mathcal D$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Elementwise saturation in the controller/evaluation interface bounds
the applied reference. The linear action head does not itself impose
the $\pi/6$ command limit.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Local Lipschitz Regularity]
\label{lem:lipschitz}
For frozen finite weights, positive normalization scales, and positive
RMSNorm regularization, $f_{\mathrm{UHRC}}$ is Lipschitz on compact
observation carry sets.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For a fixed finite number of recurrent updates, the pre-saturation
network is locally smooth, with derivatives
bounded on compact sets. Positive RMSNorm regularization prevents a
zero denominator, including at zero latent input. Its Lipschitz bound
is at most $1/\sqrt{\epsilon}$ for the unweighted RMSNorm used here.
Their composition is therefore locally Lipschitz.
Attention, gated products, and residual maps must be included in any
numerical bound.
\end{proof}
In the supplied architecture, each newly stored carry token passes
through RMSNorm and therefore has norm at most $\sqrt H$.
Finite initialized carry tokens consequently remain in a compact
token set. This is bounded memory.

\subsubsection{Cascade Interconnection: ISS and UUB}
\label{sec:cascade}

Use the fixed rate scales
$\sigma_\phi=\sigma_\theta=37.2\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$,
$\sigma_\psi=8.31\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and the interleaved state
\[
 \chi=(e_\phi,p/\sigma_\phi,e_\theta,q/\sigma_\theta,
             e_\psi,r/\sigma_\psi)^\top,\qquad
 e_i=\eta_{r,i}-\eta_i.
\]
All angles are in radians. Set
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
 P_0=\operatorname{diag}(P_{\mathrm{rp}},P_{\mathrm{rp}},P_\psi),\\
 P_{\mathrm{rp}}=\begin{bmatrix}
 4.11450013&-1.00164618\\-1.00164618&1.70383480
 \end{bmatrix},\\
 P_\psi=\begin{bmatrix}
 1.26523980&-0.25464857\\-0.25464857&1.24448025
 \end{bmatrix},\\
 0.99I_6<P_0<4.48I_6.
 \label{eq:common_sampled_certificate}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The displayed terminating decimals define the certificate matrices.
Use the norms
\[
 \|x\|_{P_0}=\sqrt{x^\top P_0x},\qquad
 \|A\|_{P_0}=\sup_{x\ne0}\frac{\|Ax\|_{P_0}}{\|x\|_{P_0}}.
\]
For every gain choice in \eqref{eq:pid_box}, the scaled nominal map
$N(K)$ and its within interval held feedback map $L_t(K)$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \|N(K)\|_{P_0}<0.9903,\qquad
 \|L_t(K)\|_{P_0}\le1,\qquad
 \|F_t\|_{P_0}<1.35,\quad0\le t\le h,
 \label{eq:common_contraction}
\end{equation}
where $F_t$ is the open, damped nominal flow.

For completeness, these uniform bounds reduce to finite matrix tests.
On axis $i$, define $d_i/J_i=\delta_i$ and
$f_i(t)=(1-e^{-\delta_i t})/\delta_i$. Then
\[
\begin{gathered}
 F_i(t)=\begin{bmatrix}1&-\sigma_i f_i(t)\\0&1-\delta_i f_i(t)\end{bmatrix},\quad
 G_i(t)=\begin{bmatrix}-(t-f_i(t))/d_i\\f_i(t)/(J_i\sigma_i)\end{bmatrix},\\
 L_i(t,K)=F_i(t)+G_i(t)[K_{p,i}\ {-K_{d,i}\sigma_i}],\qquad N_i=L_i(h,K).
\end{gathered}
\]
The pair $(t,f_i(t))$ lies in the triangle with vertices
$(0,0),(h,f_i(h)),(h,h)$.
For fixed gains, $L_i$ is affine in this pair and for fixed $(t,f)$,
it is affine in the gains. Convexity of an induced matrix norm
therefore reduces \eqref{eq:common_contraction} to gain-box vertices
and these three time vertices. The open flow uses $0\le f_i(t)\le h$.
The positive-definiteness tests for the displayed matrices were
verified with rational interval arithmetic, enclosing
$e^{-\delta_i h}$ between the nineteenth and twentieth alternating
Taylor sums. The smallest lower bound on the leading principal minor
or determinant of $0.9903^2P_i-N_i^\top P_iN_i$ is
$1.94\times10^{-4}$ at the tested vertices.

\paragraph{Quantified nonlinear residual.}
The exact Euler transformation is
\[
 \mathcal T(\phi,\theta)=
 \begin{bmatrix}
 1&\sin\phi\tan\theta&\cos\phi\tan\theta\\
 0&\cos\phi&-\sin\phi\\
 0&\sin\phi/\cos\theta&\cos\phi/\cos\theta
 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
For $|\phi|,|\theta|\le a_\eta\le0.01$, direct entrywise bounds give
\begin{equation}
 \|\mathcal T-I_3\|_2^2
 \le\tan^2a_\eta+(1+\sec^2a_\eta)
                   (a_\eta^2+a_\eta^4/4)
 <(1.75a_\eta)^2\quad(a_\eta>0).
 \label{eq:euler_residual_bound}
\end{equation}
For the axisymmetric inertia in Assumption 2,
\[
 J^{-1}(-\boldsymbol\omega_B\times J\boldsymbol\omega_B)
 =g(-qr,pr,0)^\top,\quad
 g=\frac{0.01428-0.0082}{0.0082},\quad
 \|g(-qr,pr,0)\|_2\le\tfrac g2\|\boldsymbol\omega_B\|_2^2.
\]
Let $\mathcal E u=(u_1,0,u_2,0,u_3,0)^\top$ and
$\mathcal B u=(0,u_1/(J_1\sigma_1),0,u_2/(J_2\sigma_2),
0,u_3/(J_3\sigma_3))^\top$.
The matrices in \eqref{eq:common_sampled_certificate} give
\[
 \|\mathcal E u\|_{P_0}\le2.03\|u\|_2,\quad
 \|\boldsymbol\omega_B\|_2\le30.8\|\chi\|_{P_0},\quad
 |e_\phi|,|e_\theta|\le0.533\|\chi\|_{P_0},\quad
 \|\mathcal B u\|_{P_0}\le9.5\|u\|_2.
\]
Since $\max_i\sqrt{(P_i)_{22}}/\sigma_i<0.135$ and
$0.135(g/2)(30.8)^2<48$, the normalized gyroscopic contribution
has weighted norm at most $48\|\chi\|_{P_0}^2$. Hence, for held references
$|\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}|,|\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}|\le\bar r$,
the complete kinematic and gyroscopic residual obeys
\begin{equation}
 \|\mathcal R(\chi,\boldsymbol\eta_r)\|_{P_0}
 \le111\bar r\|\chi\|_{P_0}+108\|\chi\|_{P_0}^2,
 \quad\bar r+0.533\|\chi\|_{P_0}\le0.01.
 \label{eq:nonlinear_quantified}
\end{equation}
For example, the first coefficient follows from
$2.03\times30.8\times1.75<111$, and the quadratic coefficient
from $111\times0.533+48<108$. Thus nonzero references produce
a quantified linear residual as well as quadratic terms.

\paragraph{Admissible references and invariant enclosure.}

Define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal R_{\mathrm{ad}}(\bar r,R)=\{\boldsymbol\eta_r:\;&
 \boldsymbol\eta_r(t)=\boldsymbol\eta_{r,k},\quad kh\le t<(k+1)h,\\
 &\eta_{r,k,3}=0,\quad |\eta_{r,k,1}|,|\eta_{r,k,2}|\le\bar r,\\
 &\|\boldsymbol\eta_{r,k+1}-\boldsymbol\eta_{r,k}\|_2\le R\}.
 \label{eq:admissible_references}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Let $w(t)$ be additive physical torque, with
$\sup_{kh\le t<(k+1)h}\|w(t)\|_2\le W$.
For sample radius $s$ and a larger intersample radius $S$, set
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
 \ell_R=111\bar r+108S,\quad
 d_h=1.35h\ell_R<1,\quad c_w=1.35h(9.5),\\
 a_*=0.9903+\frac{d_h}{1-d_h}<1,\qquad
 b_w=\frac{c_w}{1-d_h}.
 \label{eq:nonlinear_constants}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
Sufficient containment and input-budget conditions are
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
 \bar r+0.533S\le0.01,\qquad
 \frac{s+c_wW}{1-d_h}<S,\\
 2.03R+b_wW\le(1-a_*)s,\qquad
 15s+W<0.6,\quad7.5s+W<0.35.
 \label{eq:invariant_conditions}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
Here $15s$ and $7.5s$ bound commanded roll/pitch and yaw torque on
the sample ellipsoid uniformly over the gain box. They follow from
$\sqrt{s^2a_i^\top P_i^{-1}a_i}$,
$a_i=(K_{p,i},-K_{d,i}\sigma_i)^\top$, tested at gain vertices.

\begin{theorem}[Local Sampled Cascade ISS and UUB]
\label{thm:cascade_iss}
For the physical rotational model in Assumption 2, gains in
$\mathcal K_{\mathrm{box}}$, references in
$\mathcal R_{\mathrm{ad}}(\bar r,R)$, and bounded torque $w$, suppose
\eqref{eq:invariant_conditions} holds and $\|\chi_0\|_{P_0}\le s$.
Then the reference-dependent sample enclosure
\[
 \Omega_s=\{(\boldsymbol\eta,\boldsymbol\omega_B,
                  \boldsymbol\eta_r):\|\chi\|_{P_0}\le s,
 |\eta_{r,1}|,|\eta_{r,2}|\le\bar r,\ \eta_{r,3}=0\}
\]
is invariant at control updates, its intersample tube satisfies
$\|\chi(t;\boldsymbol\eta_{r,k})\|_{P_0}<S$, and torque saturation
is inactive. Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 \|\chi_{k+1}\|_{P_0}\le a_*\|\chi_k\|_{P_0}
 +2.03\|\delta\boldsymbol\eta_{r,k}\|_2+b_wW,
 \label{eq:full_decrease}
\end{equation}
and consequently
\begin{equation}
 \|\chi_k\|_2\le
 \sqrt{4.48/0.99}\,a_*^k\|\chi_0\|_2+
 \frac{2.03R+b_wW}{\sqrt{0.99}(1-a_*)}.
 \label{eq:nonlinear_iss_estimate}
\end{equation}
This establishes local ISS of rotational tracking, with invariant
local-domain containment, and UUB for the stated admissible inputs.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Over a held-reference interval, variation of constants gives
\[
 \chi(t)=L_t(K)\chi_k+
 \int_0^t F_{t-u}[\mathcal R(\chi(u),\boldsymbol\eta_{r,k})
                              +\mathcal B w(u)]\,du.
\]
Up to a first exit from $\|\chi\|_{P_0}<S$,
\eqref{eq:nonlinear_quantified} bounds the residual by
$\ell_R\|\chi\|_{P_0}$.
For $Y=\sup_{0\le t\le h}\|\chi(t)\|_{P_0}$,
\[
 Y\le\|\chi_k\|_{P_0}+d_hY+c_wW,
 \qquad Y\le\frac{\|\chi_k\|_{P_0}+c_wW}{1-d_h}<S.
\]
The strict tube inequality prevents a first exit; the angle bounds
and held-torque limits prevent exit from the valid model domain.
At $h$, the stronger nominal contraction in
\eqref{eq:common_contraction}, followed by the reference jump
$\mathcal E\delta\boldsymbol\eta_{r,k}$, yields
\eqref{eq:full_decrease}. The input budget gives
$\|\chi_{k+1}\|_{P_0}\le a_*s+2.03R+b_wW\le s$.
Induction proves sample invariance and tube validity; geometric-series
comparison and norm equivalence prove \eqref{eq:nonlinear_iss_estimate}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Parametric representation of the certificate.}
Using the preceding verified constants at $h=0.01\,\mathrm{s}$,
represent the admissible region and input choices by
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal A=\left\{(\bar r,s,S,R,W):
 \bar r,R,W\ge0,\quad0<s<S,\quad
 \text{\eqref{eq:nonlinear_constants} and
       \eqref{eq:invariant_conditions} hold}\right\}.
 \label{eq:admissible_certificate_family}
\end{equation}
No particular radius, reference increment, or disturbance bound is
prescribed. Each member of $\mathcal A$ defines an invariant enclosure
$\Omega_s$, an intersample tube of radius $S$, and the reference set
$\mathcal R_{\mathrm{ad}}(\bar r,R)$.
The set is nonempty, with $\bar r=R=W=0$, choose $S>0$
sufficiently small for contraction and angle containment, then choose
$0<s<(1-d_h)S$ satisfying the torque limits. The strict margins
also admit sufficiently small positive reference and disturbance bounds.

The nonlinear contraction condition has the equivalent representation
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 a_*<1
 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 d_h<\frac{1-0.9903}{2-0.9903},\qquad 0\le d_h<1,\\
 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 111\bar r+108S<
 \frac{1-0.9903}{1.35h(2-0.9903)}.
 \label{eq:parametric_contraction_condition}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Thus the available contraction margin determines the admissible
nonlinear region. For each admissible $(\bar r,s,S,W)$, the reference
increment budget is
\begin{equation}
 0\le R\le R_{\max}(\bar r,s,S,W)
 :=\frac{(1-a_*)s-b_wW}{2.03},\qquad
 b_wW\le(1-a_*)s.
 \label{eq:parametric_reference_budget}
\end{equation}
The disturbance bound must also satisfy the intersample and actuator
conditions, equivalently
\begin{equation}
 0\le W<\min\left\{
 \frac{(1-d_h)S-s}{c_w},\quad0.6-15s,\quad0.35-7.5s
 \right\}.
 \label{eq:parametric_disturbance_budget}
\end{equation}
Together with $\bar r+0.533S\le0.01$, these inequalities specify
the permissible choices rather than assigning unsupported numerical
operating limits. Initialization requires $\|\chi_0\|_{P_0}\le s$.

The corresponding physical bounds are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 |\phi(t)|,|\theta(t)|&\le\bar r+0.533S,\\
 \|\boldsymbol\omega_B(t)\|_2&\le30.8S,\\
 |\tau_{\phi,k}|,|\tau_{\theta,k}|&\le15s,
 \qquad|\tau_{\psi,k}|\le7.5s,\\
 \limsup_{k\to\infty}\|\chi_k\|_{P_0}
 &\le\frac{2.03R+b_wW}{1-a_*}\le s.
 \label{eq:parametric_physical_bounds}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here the torque bounds apply to commanded torques; the additive
torque allowance $W$ is included in
\eqref{eq:parametric_disturbance_budget}.
For zero reference increments and zero external torque, the tracking
origin is locally exponentially stable; bounded admissible inputs
give the ultimate error neighbourhood in \eqref{eq:nonlinear_iss_estimate}.
Attitude and rate bounds are recovered from $\eta_i=\eta_{r,i}-e_i$
and $\omega_i=\sigma_i\chi_{2i}$, not by feeding angle units into a
torque-input ISS gain.

\begin{remark}[Scope of the nonlinear certificate]
The family $\mathcal A$ certifies local invariant enclosures near hover,
not the full $30^\circ$ operating domain.
Each choice must satisfy
\eqref{eq:nonlinear_constants}--\eqref{eq:invariant_conditions}
The current $30^\circ$ clipping interface alone does not enforce
\eqref{eq:admissible_references}. An all-time UHRC interconnection
claim therefore requires an implemented admissibility mechanism or
an independent guarantee that its applied references belong to that set.
The altitude PID and navigational position dynamics require separate
stability arguments.
\end{remark}



\subsubsection{Translational Consequences and Convergence}
\label{sec:translation}

\begin{proposition}[Velocity UUB]
\label{prop:trans}

Under Assumption 4,
\[
 \|\mathbf v_I^{\mathrm h}(t)\|
 \le e^{-c_{\min}t/m}\|\mathbf v_I^{\mathrm h}(0)\|
 +\frac{T_{\max}}{c_{\min}}(1-e^{-c_{\min}t/m}).
\]
Thus $\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|\mathbf v_I^{\mathrm h}(t)\|
\le T_{\max}/c_{\min}$, but attitude stability and bounded velocity
alone do not guarantee bounded position error.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The horizontal force satisfies $\|F_h\|\le T_{\max}$, giving
\[
 D^+\|\mathbf v_I^{\mathrm h}\|
 \le T_{\max}/m-(c_{\min}/m)\|\mathbf v_I^{\mathrm h}\|.
\]
Scalar comparison yields the estimate. A constant nonzero horizontal
force can produce a nonzero terminal velocity and hence unbounded
position drift, providing a counterexample to a general position bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Goal-Region Attractiveness]
\label{thm:conv}
Let $V_{\mathrm{nav}}=\tfrac12\|\mathbf r_I^{\mathrm h}
-\mathbf r_{\mathrm{goal}}^{\mathrm h}\|^2$.
For $r_0>0$, assume the goal region
$\mathcal G=\{\|\mathbf r_I^{\mathrm h}
-\mathbf r_{\mathrm{goal}}^{\mathrm h}\|\le r_0\}$ is forward invariant.
If there exists $\alpha_3\in\mathcal K$ such that
\begin{equation}
 \dot V_{\mathrm{nav}}
 =(\mathbf r_I^{\mathrm h}-\mathbf r_{\mathrm{goal}}^{\mathrm h})^\top
   \mathbf v_I^{\mathrm h}
 \le-\alpha_3(V_{\mathrm{nav}})
 \label{eq:gdc}
\end{equation}
outside $\mathcal G$ for all $t\ge T$, the trajectory enters
$\mathcal G$ in finite time and remains there.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Outside $\mathcal G$,
$\dot V_{\mathrm{nav}}\le-\alpha_3(r_0^2/2)<0$.
Failure to enter would force $V_{\mathrm{nav}}$ below $r_0^2/2$
in finite time, a contradiction. Invariance ensures persistence.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
Equation \eqref{eq:gdc} is a sufficient policy-dependent condition,
not guaranteed by behavioural cloning. An obstacle detour may
temporarily violate it. Attraction to a nonzero-radius goal region
does not establish exact goal convergence, low-speed settling,
or collision avoidance. 
\end{remark}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section reports the quantitative and qualitative performance of the UHRC-PID and classical hierarchical baseline over the five test cases (TC01--TC05).
The metrics are computed from $N = 30$ Monte Carlo trials per scenario.
RMSE and IAE are evaluated over the complete duration of each episode, which depends on the time required to reach the goal, and include failed and divergent trials.
This is done so that the aggregate statistics characterize operational reliability, rather than selective best-case performance.
The PLR is the ratio of the actual path length travelled to the straight-line distance between the start and goal.
Table~\ref{tab:results_summary} summarizes the performance of both controllers across all scenarios.

\subsection{Classical Hierarchical Baseline}
\label{sec:baseline}


The classical baseline comprises four stages which implement the full supervisory-regulatory decomposition described in the literature \parencite{sanders_hierarchical_1998, scattolini_hierarchical_2007}.
It serves as a representative instance of the class of hierarchical architectures that the UHRC is intended to replace.

An Inertial Navigation System Extended Kalman Filter (INSEKF) combines Global Positioning System (GPS) position estimates with Attitude and Heading Reference System (AHRS) orientation estimates at 10\,Hz, yielding the pose estimate $[\hat{\mathbf{r}}_I, \hat{\mathbf{q}}_{BI}]$.
LiDAR odometry is obtained through Iterative Closest Point (ICP) registration of 2D point cloud keyframes.
The keyframes are accumulated in a pose graph, which is optimized using the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm at fixed intervals of 500 nodes \parencite{gupta_simultaneous_2022, milijas_comparison_2021}.

The A* algorithm then computes a collision-free path on a 2D occupancy grid, formed by projecting the LiDAR returns onto the ground plane with a cell resolution of 2.0\,m.
The grid covers a height band of $\pm 2.0$\,m about the drone's estimated flight level.
Path feasibility is guaranteed when a collision-free grid path exists \parencite{zhou_review_2021}; otherwise, the previously valid path is retained.


The planned waypoint sequence is converted into position setpoints at 10\,Hz by a pure pursuit tracker with fixed lookahead distance $L_d = 0.8$\,m \parencite{huang_path_2020}.


The position setpoints are tracked through a cascaded PID structure.
The outer position loop converts horizontal position errors into desired roll and pitch references $[\phi_d, \theta_d]$ using the standard small-angle coupling $\theta_d = a_x^{\mathrm{des}} / g$, $\phi_d = -a_y^{\mathrm{des}} / g$.
The inner attitude loop uses derivative-filtered PID controllers on each axis to convert the attitude and altitude errors into body wrench commands \parencite{ren_efficient_2025,bouabdallah_design_2007}.

\subsection{TC01: Open-Field Transit}
\label{sec:tc01}


TC01 evaluates baseline goal-seeking performance in an obstacle-free environment.
The classical controller reached the goal in all 30 trials, achieving 100\% success, with a mean RMSE of 0.938\,m and an IAE of 43.426\,m\,s.
The residual tracking error is attributable to cumulative drift in the ICP odometry subsystem and the fixed-interval pose graph optimization.
During square-trajectory validation, the INSEKF position estimator exhibited residual errors of 0.376\,m along $x$ and 0.265\,m along $y$, confirming the direct propagation of localization noise into tracking error.
The PLR of 2.090 results from lookahead-induced lateral oscillations of the pure-pursuit tracker about the nominal straight-line path.

The UHRC-PID achieved 30\% success (9 of 30 trials), with a mean RMSE of 5.307\,m and a PLR of 2.09.
In the two successful trials, the initial goal distance was below approximately 7\,m.
At this range, the policy converged to a stable goal-seeking attractor before the carry state drifted outside the training distribution.
For more distant goals, however, errors in the generated torque predictions accumulate over successive timesteps, driving the vehicle into states not previously encountered by the model.

\subsection{TC04: Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance}
\label{sec:tc04}


TC04 provides the most significant finding of the evaluation.
Obstacles are withheld from both sensor streams until $t \geq 3.0$\,s, when they are revealed during flight.
The classical controller failed in all 30 trials, giving a 0\% success rate, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tc04}.

The failure of the classical controller follows from its architecture.
Obstacle detection requires projection of the LiDAR scan into the occupancy grid, computation of a revised A* waypoint sequence and propagation of that sequence to the pure-pursuit tracker.
A delay therefore occurs between obstacle revelation and the availability of an updated reference trajectory.
During this interval, the low-level PID continues to track the previous trajectory, causing collision before replanning can redirect the vehicle, as confirmed by Figure~\ref{fig:tc04_classical}.
The delay is inherent to the fixed supervisory-regulatory interface and cannot be removed by tuning the existing architecture.

Failures of the UHRC-PID in this test case are attributable to the policy instability that dominates all test cases.
The drone diverges from its nominal path before reaching the obstacle, instead of colliding with it.
Small erratic attitude predictions appear immediately after carry initialization, consistent with the accumulation of out-of-distribution states before obstacle revelation at $t = 3.0$\,s.
Notably, however, in trials where a coherent flight path was maintained until $t = 3.0$\,s, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tc04_uhrc-pid}, the 32-ray LiDAR scan immediately registered the newly revealed obstacle, and the observation embedding was updated within the same H-L reasoning cycle.
The resulting replanning latency is bounded by a single 10\,ms inference call, compared with the 100\,ms pipeline cycle.
This direct response constitutes the principal structural advantage of unified reasoning over explicit hierarchical decomposition in dynamic scenarios.

\subsection{TC05: Parametric Robustness}
\label{sec:tc05}
TC05 retains the corridor arrangement of TC02 while simultaneously perturbing the plant parameters (mass $\pm 20\%$, diagonal inertia $\pm 30\%$, drag coefficients $\pm 50\%$).
The contrasting responses of the controllers characterize the performance-robustness boundary between learned and classical architectures.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tc05_classical}, the classical controller achieved 100\% success over all 30 perturbed trials, with a mean RMSE of 0.707\,m, an IAE of 13.346\,m\,s and a PLR of 1.227.
The metrics remain comparable to the nominal TC01 and TC03 results, demonstrating bounded performance degradation despite significant parametric variation.
The thrust output under perturbed conditions (2814.94\,Nm) was elevated relative to the nominal TC01 baseline (5136.5\,Nm), which is consistent with the integral term actively compensating for the reduced effective hover thrust.

Under TC05 conditions, the UHRC achieved 33.3\% success (10 of 30 trials), with a mean RMSE of 58.675\,m and an ITAE of 73{,}011.560\,m\,s$^2$.
Figure~\ref{fig:tc05_uhrc_compare} compares the UHRC trajectories under nominal and perturbed dynamics.
The nominal case (Figure~\ref{fig:tc05_uhrc_nominal}) shows a coherent straight-line transit, whereas 20\% model uncertainty (Figure~\ref{fig:tc05_uhrc-pid_torque}) causes the trajectory to deviate from the intended path.
The error metrics are less informative in this comparison because the trials use different initial positions and goals.
Nevertheless, the qualitative trajectory comparison shows that the perturbed parameters produce the suboptimal path.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results from TC01--TC05 reveal a consistent picture of the two control architectures.
Failures of the classical controller are architecturally deterministic, owing to the replanning delay imposed by the supervisory-regulatory interface, which cannot be reduced without redesigning the pipeline.
The UHRC-PID, meanwhile, exhibits superior reactive spatial reasoning in dynamic environments, relying on the inner-loop PID for rigid-body stabilization while the outer-loop neural network handles kinematic guidance.
Their differing modes of success and failure therefore identify complementary limits of the two designs.

\subsection{Policy Stability and Guidance Efficacy}
\label{sec:discuss_stability}

The cascaded implementation provides a proof of principle that the UHRC can achieve highly effective goal-directed navigation under nominal conditions.
The inner-loop PID performs high-frequency attitude regulation, removing the need for the outer loop to compensate for fast rotational dynamics.
By extension, this mitigates the accumulation of distributional errors \parencite{le_mero_survey_2022, block_is_2024}.

The classical inner loop supplies a continuous restoring force against transient deviations through integral correction.
In a representative open-field transit (TC01), the resulting hybrid achieved a PLR of 1.010, an IAE of 37.092\,m$\cdot$s and an RMSE of 8.362\,m.
These metrics confirm the functional correctness of the learned spatial reasoning when unbounded attitude divergence is prevented.
Thus, the spatial planning component of the UHRC has the potential for stable long-horizon closed-loop operation when decoupled from low-level action generation.



\subsection{Overcoming the Information Bottleneck: TC02 and TC03}
\label{sec:discuss_bottleneck}

The theoretical motivation for the UHRC is to resolve the information bottleneck at the classical supervisory-regulatory interface.
The A* planner supplies only a waypoint sequence to the low-level PID, discarding all contextual information associated with obstacle proximity and local geometry \parencite{kolchinsky_nonlinear_2019, abdelmaksoud_control_2020}.
The TC02 and TC03 results provide strong empirical support for this hypothesis.
Near obstacle surfaces, the classical controller oscillates laterally about the planned path.
This behavior is attributable to the pure-pursuit tracker receiving only positional waypoints, without any representation of clearance from neighboring obstacles.

The UHRC-PID trajectories exhibit qualitatively smoother clearance geometry in the constrained scenarios.
This agrees with the L-module having direct access to LiDAR returns through the unified observation vector when computing attitude intent.
During symmetric corridor transit (TC02), for example, the UHRC-PID achieved a PLR of 1.081, although the observed flight path was suboptimal relative to that of the classical controller.
During gap navigation (TC03), the corresponding PLR was 1.171 and the RMSE was 6.455\,m.
Because basic policy stability is maintained by the inner loop, the UHRC successfully demonstrates its architectural advantage in constrained geometric environments.

\subsection{Architectural Highlights in Dynamic Environments: TC04}
\label{sec:discuss_tc04}

TC04 provides the most architecturally significant result of the evaluation, demonstrating where the learned hierarchy explicitly outperforms classical explicit decomposition.
Obstacles are withheld from both controllers' sensor streams until $t \geq 3.0$\,s and are then revealed in the drone's active flight path.

The classical controller must first project the LiDAR scan into the occupancy grid, recompute the A* waypoint sequence and transmit the revised path to the pure-pursuit tracker.
The resulting delay causes collision before replanning can redirect the vehicle \parencite{li_hierarchical_2023, zhu_hierarchical_2024}.
This delay is inherent to the fixed supervisory-regulatory interface and cannot be addressed through tuning.

The UHRC-PID, however, successfully navigated the dynamic environment.
A representative successful trial achieved a PLR of 3.104, an IAE of 178.014\,m$\cdot$s and an RMSE of 9.001\,m.
The LiDAR observation was updated during the same 10\,ms inference cycle in which the obstacle was revealed, with the new geometry immediately represented in the observation embedding of the next H-L reasoning cycle.
Bounding the replanning latency by a single inference call gives unified reasoning its principal structural advantage over explicit hierarchical decomposition in dynamic scenarios.

\subsection{Distributional Fragility vs Integral Robustness: TC05}
\label{sec:discuss_robustness}

TC05 most directly characterizes the fundamental trade-off between the architectures under simultaneous perturbations of mass ($\pm 20\%$), inertia ($\pm 30\%$), drag ($\pm 50\%$) and thrust coefficient ($\pm 15\%$).

The classical controller's success demonstrates that its cascaded integral feedback is a principled and highly effective mechanism for parametric robustness.
The PID integrators treat changes in the effective plant dynamics as persistent disturbances, compensating for them continuously without requiring knowledge of their magnitude.

The UHRC-PID achieves similar performance, although some distributional fragility remains.
Training through behavioral cloning on a single nominal dynamic model calibrates the policy's implicit representation of spatial kinematics exclusively to the nominal parameter set \parencite{le_mero_survey_2022}.
Under perturbed conditions, the control inputs exhibit the expected increase in thrust, maintaining approximately 11.8\,N compared with the nominal hover thrust of 9.81\,N.
Despite the attitude bounds imposed during training, prediction errors in the attitude references still produce high-frequency torque oscillations.
The PID compensates for these errors, but the overall trajectory remains suboptimal.

Despite the uncertainties, a successful perturbed transit achieved a PLR of 1.066, an IAE of 63.861\,m$\cdot$s and an RMSE of 4.391\,m.
This shows that grounding the execution in classical PID logic anchors the degraded kinematic guidance of the HRM, providing a significantly wider stability margin even as the outer-loop predictions deteriorate.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this work, we developed the Unified Hierarchical Reasoning Controller (UHRC), constituting the first systematic application of the Hierarchical Reasoning Model (HRM) architecture to a continuous nonlinear closed-loop control system.
The model was ultimately formulated as a cascaded UHRC-PID hybrid, pairing a recurrent transformer outer loop, which generates attitude intent, with a classical inner loop for high-frequency rigid-body stabilization.
Comparison with a classical SLAM-augmented cascaded PID baseline over five structured test cases established the principal findings of the study.
Firstly, the HRM architecture can learn a highly functional continuous control policy when timescale separation is respected.
Secondly, the cascaded formulation successfully addresses the compounding errors which constitute the main limitation to reliable deployment of monolithic end-to-end networks.
Thirdly, single-step inference allows the unified architecture to bypass structural replanning delays and outperform the classical baseline in dynamic environments (TC04).
Finally, although the hybrid improves general robustness, classical integral feedback remains superior in rejecting severe, unmodelled parametric uncertainty.

Future work will primarily consider domain randomization during training, which directly addresses the remaining distributional fragility without modifying the architecture.
Replacement of the PID regulators with an ideal controller will also be investigated to improve the accuracy of the learned model.
RL fine-tuning from the behaviorally cloned initialization would further permit the discovery of corrective strategies absent from the offline expert.
Beyond these extensions, the UHRC framework provides a systematic basis for investigating whether unified hierarchical reasoning can serve as the definitive outer-loop guidance architecture for complex autonomous systems.
