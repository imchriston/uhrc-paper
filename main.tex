\begin{abstract}
The autonomous control of nonlinear systems such as quadrotor UAVs traditionally
requires an explicit separation between slow outer-loop trajectory planning and fast
inner-loop attitude stabilisation. End-to-end learning approaches attempt to collapse
this hierarchy but suffer from severe sample inefficiency and compounding covariate
shift, leading to divergence in dynamic environments. This paper presents the Unified
Hierarchical Reasoning Controller (UHRC), a novel cascaded architecture that
integrates a two-level recurrent transformer as a high-level kinematic guidance
module with a classical inner-loop PID attitude regulator. By training the UHRC to
output kinematically feasible attitude intent $[\phi_{\mathrm{ref}},
\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}]$, the architecture inherently respects the multiscale temporal
dynamics of the plant and confines the consequences of neural prediction error to the
slower translational timescale where the inner PID provides a stabilising correction.
The hybrid UHRC-PID is evaluated against a classical SLAM-augmented cascaded PID
baseline across five structured test cases of increasing geometric complexity:
open-field transit, symmetric corridor navigation, gap threading, dynamic obstacle
avoidance, and parametric robustness under simultaneous plant uncertainty. The hybrid
achieves success rates of 20--30\% across constrained navigation scenarios and 20\%
under dynamic obstacle conditions where both the pure neural policy and the classical
SLAM controller record 0\%, while providing a formal cascade ISS stability guarantee
for the rotational subsystem. These results establish the UHRC-PID as a robust,
computationally tractable framework that resolves the divergence modes of flat
end-to-end policies without sacrificing the reactive spatial reasoning advantages of
learned hierarchical control.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The autonomous control of complex nonlinear systems remains one of the most
consequential open problems in control engineering
\parencite{iqbal_nonlinear_2017, liu_new_2015, daoutidis_future_2023}.
Systems such as unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), autonomous ground vehicles (UGVs),
and high degree-of-freedom robotic manipulators are characterised by
high dimensional state spaces, nonlinear inter-state couplings, actuator
saturation, and significant parametric uncertainty
\parencite{sadati_two-level_2015, ameli_robust_2021, iqbal_nonlinear_2017,
slotine_applied_1991}.
The fundamental difficulties are that the dynamical regimes governing long horizon
navigation and those governing fast stabilisation operate on different
timescales, differ in their sensitivity to model error, and impose competing
demands on the same system.
\parencite{liang_nonlinear_2018, abdelmaksoud_control_2020}.
Designing a controller that simultaneously addresses long horizon planning and
fast reactive stabilisation across these coupled dynamics is fundamentally a
multiscale reasoning problem, and no single architecture has proven universally
adequate across the competing demands of stability, adaptability, and
computational tractability \parencite{rojas_complex_2025, goodwin_control_2001}.
 
Classical hierarchical control has long addressed this multi-scale challenge
through explicit structural decomposition, a high-level supervisory layer
generates strategic plans and setpoints while a low-level regulatory layer
executes fast stabilisation and trajectory tracking
\parencite{singh_systems_1978, sadati_two-level_2015, scattolini_hierarchical_2007,
mesarovic_two_1970, wilson_foundations_1979}.
The theoretical underpinnings of this separation trace to the multilevel systems
framework of \parencite{mesarovic_two_1970} and the hierarchical decomposition
methods formalised in \parencite{singh_systems_1978, siljak_decentralized_2013},
which established that large scale optimisation problems can be rendered
tractable by decomposing them into coordinated subproblems across levels.
This architecture has achieved sustained success across aerospace, process
control, and robotics, benefiting from well established stability theory,
modular implementation, and interpretable failure modes
\parencite{sanders_hierarchical_1998, wang_hierarchical_2024, liu_generalized_2016,
bahnasawi_decentralised_1990, roberts_optimal_2001}.
The quadrotor UAV exemplifies this paradigm directly, the natural timescale
separation between slow translational dynamics and fast rotational dynamics
motivates a cascaded structure in which outer loop position planners generate
attitude references consumed by inner-loop attitude regulators
\parencite{hoffmann_quadrotor_2007, liang_nonlinear_2018, bouabdallah_pid_2004,
nagaty_control_2013, abdelhay_modeling_2019}.
Model predictive control has provided a principled realisation of the
supervisory layer in such architectures, enabling constraint aware planning
with formal stability guarantees at the coordination level
\parencite{scattolini_hierarchical_2007, wang_hierarchical_2024,
torrente_data-driven_2021}.
 
However, the fixed interface between layers introduces a structural information
bottleneck that becomes consequential as task complexity grows
\parencite{kolchinsky_nonlinear_2019, li_hierarchical_2023,
abdelmaksoud_control_2020}.
The high-level planner typically transmits heavily compressed information
ordinarily a kinematic waypoint sequence to the low-level regulator,
discarding contextual information regarding obstacle proximity, actuator
feasibility, and local geometric constraints that may be critical for downstream
control quality \parencite{zhu_hierarchical_2024, zhou_two-level_2017,
liu_adaptive_2013}.
Conversely, the low-level regulator executes reference trajectories without
access to the global context that motivated them, rendering local disturbance
responses globally suboptimal
\parencite{nguyen_identifying_2021, daoutidis_decomposition_2019}.
Fixed rule based supervisory frameworks also become brittle when the system
encounters scenarios not anticipated at design time
\parencite{sadati_hierarchical_2008, dave_design_2023}.
These limitations are not incidental but structural, they arise from the
requirement that the planner and regulator communicate through a
prespecified, fixed dimensional interface whose information content is
determined at design time rather than adapted online to task demands
\parencite{kolchinsky_nonlinear_2019, zhang_multi-level_2015}.
 
Learning based approaches have emerged as a principled alternative.
End-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) enables joint optimisation of planning
and regulation without manual interface specification
\parencite{almahamid_reinforcement_2021, levine_end--end_2016, kiran_deep_2022,
shakya_reinforcement_2023}.
Behavioural cloning provides a supervised alternative that sidesteps the
credit assignment challenges of RL by learning directly from expert
demonstrations, and has seen substantial success in autonomous driving and
manipulation \parencite{levine_end--end_2016, le_mero_survey_2022, ly_learning_2021,
block_is_2024}.
However, flat end-to-end policies are notoriously sample inefficient,
sensitive to reward shaping, and do not naturally maintain the multiscale
temporal structure that long horizon UAV navigation demands and
allocates identical computational depth regardless of whether the task requires
strategic deliberation or reactive stabilisation
\parencite{almahamid_reinforcement_2021, agyei_deep_2025}.
Deep reinforcement learning approaches applied directly to quadrotor control
have demonstrated impressive agility in simulation
\parencite{trad_real-time_2024, saviolo_learning_2023}, but the sim-to-real gap,
sample complexity, and absence of formal stability guarantees remain open
challenges \parencite{dai_lyapunov-stable_2021, agyei_deep_2025}.
Hybrid neurosymbolic and fuzzy integrated controllers partially address this
brittleness by pairing learned components with structured decomposition
\parencite{tang_fuzzy_2024, mahmoud_fractional-order_2024, tran_hybrid_2021,
baheri_hierarchical_2025}, yet the supervisory-regulatory interface and its
dimensionality remain manually prescribed.
The boundary between planning and regulation is fixed by the designer, not
learned from data \parencite{borri_design_2019, yu_survey_2023}.
 
Recent advances in neural reasoning architectures offer a substantially
different approach to this problem.
Test time scaling methods that iterate computations in latent space
\parencite{geiping_scaling_2025, li_seek_2025} and recurrent architectures that
adapt computational depth to task difficulty
\parencite{geiping_scaling_2025, li_system_2025}
suggest that the depth of processing required for a given decision can be made
emergent rather than fixed.
The Hierarchical Reasoning Model (HRM), introduced by
\parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025}, is a two tier recurrent architecture comprising
a high-level module responsible for slow, abstract planning and a low-level
module handling rapid, detailed computation, coupled through a shared carry
state that enables adaptive allocation of computational depth.
Trained on as few as 1{,}000 examples without pre-training or chain-of-thought
supervision, the HRM achieves near perfect accuracy on complex Sudoku problems
and optimal pathfinding in large maze tasks on which state-of-the-art large
language model approaches fail entirely \parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025,
huang_towards_2023, feng_efficient_2025}.
Crucially, the hierarchical role specialisation between modules emerges through
training rather than being architecturally prescribed, mirroring the
representational dimensionality hierarchy observed across cortical regions in
the mammalian brain \parencite{badre_frontal_2018, huntenburg_large-scale_2018,
murray_hierarchy_2014}, where higher order areas operate over longer intrinsic
timescales and higher dimensional representations than primary sensory regions.
Building on this, \parencite{jin_decoupled_2025} demonstrated that decoupling
planning from execution in a hierarchical framework yields measurable gains in
multistep reasoning tasks, further motivating the application of this design
principle to closed loop control.
The hierarchical neuro-symbolic decision transformer of
\parencite{baheri_hierarchical_2025} similarly demonstrates that coupling a symbolic
planner with a learned low-level policy through a structured interface
outperforms both purely symbolic and purely neural baselines on sequential
decision tasks, providing additional evidence that the hierarchical
planning-execution separation confers a genuine computational advantage beyond
what either component achieves in isolation.
 
Nevertheless, all demonstrated HRM applications to date have been confined to
discrete, turn based domains.
Whether the same unified reasoning mechanism can function as a closed loop
controller for continuous, real time nonlinear dynamical systems where
control outputs must be physically continuous, observations arrive as dense
sensory streams, and temporal context must persist across thousands of control
cycles remains entirely unexplored in the literature.
Extending HRMs to such settings requires nontrivial architectural adaptations, discrete
token outputs must be replaced by continuous commands, and the supervised
training signal must be derived from a physically meaningful expert policy
rather than symbolic ground truth labels.
Localisation and mapping, which are prerequisite for any meaningful planning
computation, must be supplied by an onboard sensor
\parencite{gupta_simultaneous_2022, milijas_comparison_2021,
munguia_uav_2025, chen_slam_2022}
whose outputs form part of the continuous observation stream consumed by
the controller.
 
This paper addresses that gap by proposing and evaluating the \textit{Unified
Hierarchical Reasoning Controller} (UHRC) in a cascaded hybrid architecture for the
autonomous navigation of a six-degree-of-freedom quadrotor UAV, constituting the
first systematic application of the HRM architecture to a continuous nonlinear
closed-loop control system. The UHRC adapts the HRM's two-level reasoning structure
into a recurrent transformer that maps a 45-dimensional body-frame observation
comprising kinematic state, goal displacement, and a 32-ray planar LiDAR scan 
to desired attitude references $[\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}, \theta_{\mathrm{ref}}]$,
trained via behavioural cloning \parencite{levine_end--end_2016, le_mero_survey_2022,
block_is_2024} on expert trajectory data generated by an A* path planner
\parencite{lavalle_planning_2006, qin_review_2023} across 500 simulated episodes.
These references are consumed by a classical cascaded PID attitude regulator, which
provides formal inner-loop stability and integral disturbance rejection. The composite
architecture is evaluated against a classical hierarchical baseline incorporating
SLAM-based localisation \parencite{gupta_simultaneous_2022, milijas_comparison_2021},
A* path planning, and cascaded PID control \parencite{bouabdallah_pid_2004,
ren_cascade_2016, abdelhay_modeling_2019, lopez-sanchez_pid_2023} across five
structured test cases of increasing environmental complexity: open-field transit
(TC01), symmetric corridor navigation (TC02), gap threading (TC03), dynamic obstacle
avoidance (TC04), and parametric robustness under simultaneous plant model uncertainty
(TC05).

The primary contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The first empirical demonstration of an HRM-based architecture operating
          as a closed-loop controller for a continuous nonlinear dynamic system, and
          the first application of this architecture in a hybrid cascaded
          configuration with a classical inner-loop regulator.

    \item A UHRC-PID hybrid architecture in which the neural outer loop produces
          bounded, smooth attitude references consumed by a PD attitude regulator,
          decoupling spatial reasoning from low-level stabilisation and resolving the
          compounding error instability of flat end-to-end behavioural cloning.

    \item A formal stability analysis establishing inner-loop local exponential
          stability via Lyapunov synthesis, ISS of the forced rotational subsystem,
          and cascade UUB of the rotational state, with explicit identification of
          the alignment condition required for goal convergence.

    \item A rigorous comparative empirical evaluation quantifying the
          performance-robustness trade-offs across all five test cases, demonstrating
          a 20\% success rate under dynamic obstacle conditions where both the pure
          neural policy and the classical SLAM controller fail, and 20--30\% success
          in constrained geometric environments.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:method} details the UHRC-PID architecture, observation

Section~\ref{sec:stability} presents the formal cascade stability analysis.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents quantitative results across all five test cases.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} analyses the observed performance-robustness trade-off

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

This section details the UHRC architecture, the classical hierarchical baseline against
which it is evaluated, the expert data generation procedure, the behavioural cloning
training pipeline, and the comparative evaluation protocol. All controllers share an
identical 6-DOF quadrotor plant, described first.

\subsection{Quadrotor Dynamics Model}
\label{sec:dynamics}

The benchmark plant is a rigid-body quadrotor modelled in the East-North-Up (ENU)
inertial frame $\mathcal{I}$ with body frame $\mathcal{B}$ fixed to the vehicle centre
of mass. The state vector is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x} = \bigl[\mathbf{r}_I^{\top},\;
                        \mathbf{v}_I^{\top},\;
                        \mathbf{q}_{BI}^{\top},\;
                        \boldsymbol{\omega}_B^{\top},\;
                        \boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top} \bigr]^{\top}
    \in \mathbb{R}^{17},
    \label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{r}_I \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and $\mathbf{v}_I \in \mathbb{R}^3$ are the
inertial position and velocity, $\mathbf{q}_{BI} \in \mathbb{R}^4$ is the unit quaternion
representing the rotation from $\mathcal{I}$ to $\mathcal{B}$ satisfying $\|\mathbf{q}_{BI}\| = 1$, $\boldsymbol{\omega}_B
\in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the angular velocity expressed in $\mathcal{B}$, and
$\boldsymbol{\Omega} \in \mathbb{R}^4$ contains the four rotor speeds. However, the rotor speeds are included purely for measurement.

The control input is taken as the body-frame wrench
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{u} = \bigl[T,\; \tau_x,\; \tau_y,\; \tau_z\bigr]^{\top}
    \in \mathbb{R}^4,
\end{equation}
comprising the net collective thrust $T$ along the body $+z$ axis and roll, pitch, and
yaw torques $\tau_x$, $\tau_y$, $\tau_z$ respectively. This formulation bypasses
explicit rotor speed dynamics and assumes ideal wrench actuation, which is appropriate
for the simulation study conducted here  \cite{hoffmann_quadrotor_2007}.

The translational dynamics are governed by Newton's second law in the inertial frame:
\begin{equation}
    m\dot{\mathbf{v}}_I =
        mg +
        R_{IB}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ T\end{bmatrix} +
        C_d^{\text{lin}}\,\mathbf{v}_I,
    \label{eq:trans}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the vehicle mass, $g$ is gravitational acceleration,
$R_{IB} = R_{BI}^{\top}$ is the rotation matrix from $\mathcal{B}$ to $\mathcal{I}$
derived from $\mathbf{q}_{BI}$, and $C_d^{\text{lin}} = \mathrm{diag}(c_x, c_y, c_z)$
is the linear aerodynamic drag matrix. The rotational dynamics follow the
Newton-Euler equations in the body frame:
\begin{equation}
    J\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_B =
        \boldsymbol{\tau}_B -
        \boldsymbol{\omega}_B \times J\boldsymbol{\omega}_B -
        C_d^{\text{rot}}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_B,
    \label{eq:rot}
\end{equation}
where $J = \mathrm{diag}(I_{xx}, I_{yy}, I_{zz})$ is the diagonal inertia matrix,
$\boldsymbol{\tau}_B = [\tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z]^{\top}$, and
$C_d^{\text{rot}} = \mathrm{diag}(d_x, d_y, d_z)$ is the rotational damping matrix.
Attitude kinematics are propagated via the unit quaternion differential equation
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\mathbf{q}}_{BI} =
        \tfrac{1}{2}\,(\mathbf{q}_{BI})\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_B,
\end{equation}
where $(\mathbf{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times3}$ is the standard quaternion kinematic
matrix \parencite{shastry_development_2018}. The system is integrated using a
fourth-order Runge-Kutta scheme at a fixed timestep of $\Delta t = 0.01$\,s, with
quaternion renormalisation applied after each step. Physical parameters are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:quad_params}.

\subsection{Classical Hierarchical Baseline}
\label{sec:baseline}


The baseline is a four-stage classical hierarchical controller implementing the full
supervisory-regulatory decomposition described in the literature
\parencite{sanders_hierarchical_1998, scattolini_hierarchical_2007}. It serves as a
representative instance of the architecture class the UHRC is designed to replace.

An Inertial Navigation System Extended Kalman Filter (INSEKF) fuses GPS position and
Attitude and Heading Reference System (AHRS) orientation estimates at 10\,Hz to
produce a pose estimate $[\hat{\mathbf{r}}_I, \hat{\mathbf{q}}_{BI}]$. LiDAR
odometry is provided by an Iterative Closest Point (ICP) registration pipeline
operating on 2D point cloud keyframes, accumulated into a pose graph optimised via
Levenberg-Marquardt at fixed intervals of 500 nodes \parencite{gupta_simultaneous_2022,
milijas_comparison_2021}.

A collision-free path is computed by the A* algorithm on a 2D occupancy grid
constructed by projecting LiDAR returns onto the ground plane at 2.0\,m cell
resolution. The grid spans the drone's estimated flight level within a height band
of $\pm 2.0$\,m. Path feasibility is guaranteed if a collision-free grid path exists
\parencite{zhou_review_2021}; otherwise the controller holds the previously valid path.


A pure pursuit tracker with a fixed lookahead distance of $L_d = 0.8$\,m translates
the planned waypoint sequence into position setpoints at 10\,Hz
\parencite{huang_path_2020}.


A cascaded PID structure realises the position setpoints. The outer position loop
maps horizontal position errors to desired roll and pitch references
$[\phi_d, \theta_d]$ via the standard small-angle coupling
$\theta_d = a_x^{\mathrm{des}} / g$, $\phi_d = -a_y^{\mathrm{des}} / g$.
The inner attitude loop generates body wrench commands from attitude and altitude
errors using derivative-filtered PID controllers on each axis
\parencite{ren_efficient_2025,bouabdallah_design_2007}.


\subsection{UHRC Architecture}
\label{sec:uhrc_arch}

The UHRC-PID is a two-level recurrent transformer that adapts the HRM architecture
\parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025} to continuous closed-loop control. Planning and stabilization are decoupled, where the UHRC and PID handles respectively.
At each timestep, the unified modules of the UHRC generate a 2-dimensional attidtude reference encoding strategic
intent. These subgoals are then fed into the lower level PID, which then produce the required torque wrench. 
In the UHRC, the modules maintain persistent memory through a rolling carry buffer that replaces the HRM's per-problem hidden state,
enabling temporal reasoning across the full episode horizon.

The UHRC maps the 45-dimensional body-frame observation $\mathbf{o}$ to a commanded attitude intent vector:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ref}} = [\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}, \theta_{\mathrm{ref}}]^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^2.
\end{equation}
The yaw reference is explicitly excluded ($\psi_{\mathrm{ref}} = 0$) because the body-frame observations are heading-invariant and the expert policy strictly commands zero yaw during transit. Furthermore, the hover thrust bias is initialized to $0.0$, as the model directly predicts angular deviations rather than thrust forces.

This attitude intent is subsequently passed to a classical inner-loop PID regulator. The inner loop computes the necessary body wrench $\mathbf{u} = [T, \tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z]^{\top}$ to track the UHRC's commanded roll and pitch, while simultaneously regulating altitude and yaw:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_i(t) = K_{p,i} e_i(t) + K_{i,i} \int_{0}^{t} e_i(\tau) d\tau + K_{d,i} \frac{d e_i(t)}{dt}, \quad i \in \{\phi, \theta\}
\end{equation}
where $e_\phi(t) = \phi_{\mathrm{ref}}(t) - \phi(t)$ and $e_\theta(t) = \theta_{\mathrm{ref}}(t) - \theta(t)$. 

By confining the neural network to the outer loop, the UHRC effectively bypasses the high-frequency stabilization task. This ensures that the generated spatial trajectories are translated into dynamically feasible actuator commands by the inner-loop PID, structurally guaranteeing local asymptotic stability of the attitude dynamics while retaining the multi-scale spatial reasoning of the HRM.


\subsubsection{Observation Representation}
\label{sec:obs}

All observations are expressed in the body frame $\mathcal{B}$. This choice renders
the observation invariant to global heading and position, reducing the domain of the
mapping the controller must learn: an identical avoidance manoeuvre is appropriate
regardless of the vehicle's absolute world-frame orientation \parencite{liang_nonlinear_2018}.
The 45-dimensional observation vector is

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{o} =
    \bigl[
        \underbrace{\mathbf{v}_B^{\top}}_{\times 3},\;
        \underbrace{\boldsymbol{\omega}_B^{\top}}_{\times 3},\;
        \underbrace{\mathbf{g}_B^{\top}}_{\times 3},\;
        \underbrace{\Delta\mathbf{r}_B^{\top}}_{\times 3},\;
        \underbrace{d_{\mathrm{norm}}}_{\times 1},\;
        \underbrace{\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{lidar}}^{\top}}_{\times 32}
    \bigr]^{\top}
    \in \mathbb{R}^{45},
    \label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}_B = R_{BI}\mathbf, {v}_I$ is the body-frame translational velocity,
$\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$ is the angular velocity, $\mathbf{g}_B = R_{BI}[0, 0, -g]^{\top}$
isthe gravity vector,
$\Delta\mathbf{r}_B = R_{BI}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{goal}} - \mathbf{r}_I)$ is the
goal displacement clipped to a maximum range $r_{\max}^{\mathrm{goal}} = 20$\,m,
$d_{\mathrm{norm}} = \min(\|\Delta\mathbf{r}_B\|, 20) / 20$ is the normalised
goal distance, and $\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{lidar}} \in [0, 1]^{32}$ is a 32-ray
planar LiDAR scan with field of view $\pi$\,rad and maximum range 5.0\,m,
normalised to the unit interval.

The separate scalar $d_{\mathrm{norm}}$ is included deliberately, without it,
the goal displacement vector $\Delta\mathbf{r}_B$ loses magnitude information as the
drone approaches the goal, causing the controller to overshoot. At runtime, the
observation is standardised by precomputed dataset statistics before being passed
to the model.


\subsubsection{Dual-Stream Input Encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}

The 13 scalar kinematic features $[\mathbf{v}_B^{\top}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_B^{\top},
\mathbf{g}_B^{\top}, \Delta\mathbf{r}_B^{\top}, d_{\mathrm{norm}}]$ and the 32-ray
LiDAR scan are processed by separate encoder branches before fusion.

A two-layer MLP first projects the 13-dimensional input to
$\mathbb{R}^{h/2}$ through a scaled hyperbolic tangent:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{s} = W_2 \tanh\!\bigl(\sqrt{H}\cdot W_1 \mathbf{o}_{\mathrm{kin}} + \mathbf{b}_1\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $h = 128$ is the model hidden dimension. The scaling factor $\sqrt{H}$
expands the effective dynamic range of kinematic inputs into the region where $\tanh$
provides informative gradients, preventing early saturation during training.

The 32 normalised range returns are processed as a 1D signal
by a two-stage convolutional encoder:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{l} = W_{\mathrm{proj}}\,
        \mathrm{Flatten}\!\bigl(
            \sigma\!\bigl(\mathrm{Conv}_{k=3}^{16}(
                \sigma(\mathrm{Conv}_{k=5}^{16}(\mathbf{o}_{\mathrm{lidar}})))\bigr)
        \bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the SiLU activation, $\mathrm{Conv}_{k}^{c}$ denotes a
1D convolution with $c$ output channels and kernel size $k$ (same padding), and
$W_{\mathrm{proj}} \in \mathbb{R}^{(h/2) \times 512}$ projects the flattened
$16 \times 32 = 512$ feature map to $\mathbb{R}^{h/2}$.

The two $h/2$-dimensional branch outputs are concatenated and
linearly projected to a single embedding $\mathbf{e}_t = W_f[\mathbf{s}; \mathbf{l}]
\in \mathbb{R}^{h}$ representing the current observation at timestep $t$.


\subsubsection{Transformer Reasoning Blocks}
\label{sec:blocks}

Both the $H$-module and $L$-module are composed of stacked \texttt{UHRCBlock} layers,
each implementing a standard pre-normalised transformer encoder block:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{z}' &= \mathrm{RMSNorm}\bigl(\mathbf{z} + \mathrm{MHA}(\mathbf{z})\bigr),
    \label{eq:attn_block} \\
    \mathbf{z}'' &= \mathrm{RMSNorm}\bigl(\mathbf{z}' + \mathrm{SwiGLU}(\mathbf{z}')\bigr),
    \label{eq:mlp_block}
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{MHA}(\cdot)$ is non-causal multi-head self-attention with
$n_h = 4$ heads and head dimension $d_h = h / n_h = 32$, and $\mathrm{SwiGLU}(\cdot)$
is the gated feed-forward network
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SwiGLU}(\mathbf{z}) =
        W_{\downarrow}\bigl(\mathrm{SiLU}(W_g \mathbf{z}) \odot W_u \mathbf{z}\bigr),
\end{equation}
with intermediate dimension $d_{\mathrm{ff}} = 512$. Non-causal attention is appropriate here because the carry
represents a temporal history rather than an autoregressive prediction task, each
position can attend to all other positions, including more recent ones.

Rotary Positional Encoding (RoPE) is applied independently to the query and key
matrices within each attention head, encoding the relative age of each position in
the carry buffer. For a sequence of length $T$ and head dimension $d_h$, the
rotary frequencies are
\begin{equation}
    \theta_i = \frac{1}{10000^{2i/d_h}}, \quad i = 0, 1, \ldots, d_h/2 - 1,
\end{equation}
RoPE is preferred over learned absolute
positional embeddings because it generalises to sequence positions not seen during
training \parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025}.


\subsubsection{Persistent Temporal Carry Buffer}
\label{sec:carry}

In the original HRM, hidden states $\mathbf{z}_H$ and $\mathbf{z}_L$ are
re-initialised for each input problem. In closed-loop control, the controller must
maintain situational awareness across successive timesteps, tracking velocity history,
recognising developing obstacle geometries, and anticipating trajectory evolution.
The UHRC therefore treats the hidden states as persistent rolling buffers:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_{H}, \mathbf{z}_{L} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times h},
\end{equation}
where $B$ is the batch size (1 at inference) and $C = 16$ is the carry length
. At each timestep, the new observation embedding
$\mathbf{e}_t$ is appended to the buffer and the oldest entry is discarded:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{roll\_carry}(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{e}_t) =
        \bigl[\mathbf{z}_{:,1:C,:};\; \mathbf{e}_t^{\top}\bigr]
    \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times h},
\end{equation}
providing a fixed-length sliding window of temporal context without unbounded memory
growth. 


\subsubsection{H-L Mutual Refinement and One-Step Gradient Approximation}
\label{sec:hl_cycles}

The $H$- and $L$-modules interact through $H_\mathrm{cyc} = 2$ outer cycles each
containing $L_\mathrm{cyc} = 2$ inner $L$-module passes. At each step, the full
sequence $\mathbf{z}_H^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 17 \times h}$ is formed by
concatenating the carry tokens with the current observation embedding:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_H^{(t)} = \bigl[\mathbf{Z}_H;\; \mathbf{e}_t^{\top}\bigr], \quad
    \mathbf{z}_L^{(t)} = \bigl[\mathbf{Z}_L;\; \mathbf{e}_t^{\top}\bigr].
\end{equation}
An observation re-injection tensor
$\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{obs}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 17 \times h}$ is constructed
with zeros at carry positions and $\mathbf{e}_t$ at position 16, ensuring the raw
observation is available as an additive bias at every $L$-module call \parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025}.

To bound the gradient graph depth and maintain training stability, the UHRC adopts
the one-step gradient approximation mirrored in \parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025}. all but the final module call in
the refinement cycle are computed under \texttt{torch.no\_grad()}, and only a single
$L$-pass followed by a single $H$-pass contribute to the gradient. 


\subsubsection{Subgoal Generation, Gated Injection, and Attitude R Output}
\label{sec:subgoal}

Following the final $H$-module update, the terminal hidden state $\mathbf{z}_H[:,16,:] \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times h}$ (the most recent position in the carry sequence) is decoded by a two-layer MLP planning head into a 2-dimensional body-frame horizontal velocity subgoal:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}} = W_2\,\mathrm{SiLU}(W_1 \mathbf{z}_H[:,16,:]) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 2}.
\end{equation}
This subgoal encodes the H-module's strategic intent as a desired velocity vector $[v_x, v_y]$ in the body frame, providing a physically interpretable interface between the two modules. The vertical component $v_z$ is deliberately omitted, as altitude tracking is effectively decoupled and governed by the independent altitude regulator.

The subgoal is injected into the $L$-module through a gated additive mechanism designed to modulate the reactive representation without overriding it:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{g} &= \sigma\bigl(W_g \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}} + \mathbf{b}_g\bigr) \in (0,1)^{B \times h}, \label{eq:gate} \\
    \mathbf{c} &= \bigl(W_p \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}}\bigr) \odot \mathbf{g} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times h}, \label{eq:context}
\end{align}
where $\sigma$ is the element-wise sigmoid. The gate bias is initialised to $\mathbf{b}_g = +2.0$, so that $\sigma(+2) \approx 0.88$ at the start of training, keeping the gate mostly open and allowing the subgoal signal to propagate freely before the planning head has converged. The gated context is broadcast across the carry sequence and the $L$-module performs a final two-layer attention pass:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_L^{\mathrm{final}} = \mathcal{L}\bigl(\mathbf{z}_L + \mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{17}^{\top}\bigr),
\end{equation}
from which the attitude reference action is decoded from the terminal position:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{u}} = W_a\,\mathbf{z}_L^{\mathrm{final}}[:,16,:] = [\hat{\phi}_{\mathrm{ref}},\; \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{ref}}]^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{2}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Expert Data Generation}
\label{sec:datagen}


Training data are generated by rolling out an expert policy comprising an A* path
planner, a pure-pursuit tracker, and the cascaded PID attitude controller on 500
successful simulated episodes at $\Delta t = 0.01$\,s per step
\parencite{levine_end--end_2016, le_mero_survey_2022}.


At the start of each episode, a 2D occupancy grid is constructed at a resolution of
$r_{\mathrm{grid}} = 0.25$\,m per cell, spanning the convex hull of the start
position, goal position, and all obstacle extents with a 2.0\,m margin. Circular
obstacles are inflated by $r_{\mathrm{inf}} = 0.25$\,m to account for vehicle body
radius. The A* algorithm searches an 8-connected neighbourhood using the octile
heuristic, guaranteeing the shortest collision-free path if one exists
\parencite{zhou_review_2021}. Episodes for which no path exists are discarded before
simulation begins.


The planned path is smoothed by three passes of a cubic averaging filter and tracked
by a pure-pursuit controller with lookahead $L_d = 1.0$\,m and maximum speed
$V_{\max} = 2.0$\,m/s. Speed is linearly ramped down to $0.3$\,m/s within 2\,m of
the goal. Upon entering a $0.1$\,m goal acceptance radius, the tracker issues a zero
velocity command for 150 additional steps (1.5\,s), demonstrating the hover
termination behaviour to the model.


To promote policy generalisation beyond the default $+x$-direction transit, six
episode categories are sampled according to the distribution in
Table~\ref{tab:episode_types}.

\begin{table}[ht]

\label{tab:episode_types}
\begin{tabular}{lll}

Type & Proportion & Description \\
\hline

The omnidirectional episodes are critical for eliminating the directional bias
intrinsic to the dominant $+x$-transit training distribution: without them, the
learned policy fails to navigate in the $-x$ or $\pm y$ directions.

Every 50 steps with probability 0.3, the current state receives a Gaussian velocity
perturbation ($\sigma = 0.2$\,m/s) and a small random yaw offset
($|\delta\psi| \leq 0.15$\,rad). This DAgger-style augmentation implemented by \cite{levine_end--end_2016},
exposes the expert to a wider distribution of states and prevents the compounding
error pathology characteristic of pure behavioural cloning.


Episodes are included only if: (i) the drone reached the goal without collision;
(ii) the goal distance decreased monotonically over coarse 100-step windows; and
(iii) the average velocity subgoal magnitude during the final hover phase was below
$0.3$\,m/s. To render the expert signal learnable by the neural policy, post-processing is applied to the raw A* tracker outputs. High-frequency discontinuities in the expert commands are smoothed using an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) filter with smoothing factors $\alpha_{\mathrm{act}} = 0.12$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{sub}} = 0.08$. 
Furthermore, to demonstrate stable termination, a hover deadband override is enforced, clamping $\phi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to zero when the vehicle enters the 0.1\,m goal acceptance radius. 
Maximum attitude commands are strictly clamped to $\pm 30^{\circ}$ to guarantee the demonstrations remain within the feasible limits of the low-level PID.

\subsection{Behavioural Cloning Training}
\label{sec:training}


\subsubsection{Dataset Construction}

The \texttt{ControlDataset} constructs sequence windows of length
$T_{\mathrm{seq}} = 8$ timesteps from the stored episode data. Windows never cross
episode boundaries. Windows that begin within the first $T_{\mathrm{seq}} - 1$ steps
of an episode are left-padded with zeros. The 45-dimensional observations are
$z$-scored using dataset-wide mean and standard deviation statistics. Attitude references
 are retained in their raw physical units. The dataset is split into training and validation subsets by
episode, with 10\% of episodes reserved for validation.

\subsubsection{Loss Function}
\label{sec:loss_function}

The total loss is a weighted sum of the action and subgoal tracking errors, modulated by a spatial proximity multiplier:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \gamma_{\mathrm{spat}} \Bigl( \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}} + \alpha_s \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sub}} + \alpha_t \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{trk}} \Bigr),
\end{equation}
where the loss hyperparameters are set to $\alpha_s = 0.1$ and $\alpha_t = 0.0$. The spatial weighting factor, $\gamma_{\mathrm{spat}}$, applies a $10\times$ penalty multiplier 
when the vehicle falls within a normalized distance threshold of the goal.


The action imitation loss is a weighted Mean Squared Error (MSE) on the attitude vector:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}} = 
        \Bigl\langle 
            \bigl(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{ref}} - \mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ref}}^*\bigr)^{\!\top} 
            \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{w}_a) 
            \bigl(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{ref}} - \mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ref}}^*\bigr) 
        \Bigr\rangle,
\end{equation}
with the normalized action weights initialized evenly to $\mathbf{w}_a = [10.0, 10.0]$.
The subgoal supervision loss evaluates the intermediate horizontal velocity intent identically:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sub}} = 
        \Bigl\langle 
            \bigl(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}} - \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{sub}}^*\bigr)^{\!\top} 
            \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{w}_s) 
            \bigl(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}} - \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{sub}}^*\bigr) 
        \Bigr\rangle,
\end{equation}
with corresponding subgoal weights $\mathbf{w}_s = [10.0, 10.0]$.

\subsection{Comparative Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval_protocol}


Both controllers are evaluated across five structured test cases (TC01--TC05) of
increasing environmental complexity. To ensure a fair comparison, both controllers
receive ideal state feedback from the same simulation state. Each
test case is repeated for $N = 30$ randomised Monte Carlo trials and metrics are
averaged over successful and failed trials.

\textbf{TC01 -- Open Transit.} Straight-line goal-seeking in an obstacle-free
environment, establishing baseline trajectory tracking performance.

\textbf{TC02 -- Symmetric Corridor.} Six obstacles arranged symmetrically around the
nominal flight path, testing multi-obstacle avoidance and path quality under spatial
constraint.

\textbf{TC03 -- Gap Navigation.} Two obstacles forming a 3\,m gap directly in the
nominal flight path, assessing control precision and gap-threading capability.

\textbf{TC04 -- Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance.} Obstacles are excluded from the
controller's sensor data until $t \geq 3.0$\,s, then revealed, requiring mid-flight
reactive replanning. Both controllers receive identical sensor data at each timestep.

\textbf{TC05 -- Parametric Robustness.} The TC02 obstacle configuration is retained
but the plant dynamics are perturbed: mass $\pm 20$\%, diagonal inertia $\pm 30$\%,
drag coefficients $\pm 50$\%, and thrust coefficient $k_T$ $\pm 15$\%. This
characterises each controller's robustness margin under distributional shift.

The primary performance metrics are root mean square error (RMSE) of the Euclidean
position error, integral absolute error (IAE), integral time-weighted absolute error
(ITAE), path length ratio (PLR = actual path length / straight-line distance) and episode
success rate (goal reached within 0.5\,m without collision).


\subsection{Stability Analysis}
\label{sec:stability}

\subsubsection{Standing Assumptions}

Let $\boldsymbol{\eta} = [\phi,\theta,\psi]^{\top}$ denote the Euler attitude,
$\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$ the body rate, and
$\boldsymbol{\eta}_r = [\phi_{\mathrm{ref}},\theta_{\mathrm{ref}},0]^{\top}$ the
commanded attitude.

\begin{description}
  \item[Assumption 1:] $\boldsymbol{\eta} \in \mathcal{D}_\eta = \{|\phi|,|\theta| \le \pi/6\}$,
    on which $\sin x = x + \mathcal{O}(x^3)$ and
    $\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}} = \boldsymbol{\omega}_B + \mathcal{O}(\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|\|\boldsymbol{\omega}_B\|)$.
  \item[Assumption 2:] 
    $\boldsymbol{\omega}_B \times J\boldsymbol{\omega}_B = \mathcal{O}(\|\boldsymbol{\omega}_B\|^2)$,
    a locally Lipschitz perturbation vanishing at the origin.
  \item[Assumption 3:] $\boldsymbol{\eta}_r(\cdot)$ is absolutely continuous with
    $\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_r\|_\infty \le \dot{\eta}_{\max}$,
    $\|\ddot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_r\|_\infty \le \ddot{\eta}_{\max}$.
  \item[Assumption 4:] $T(t) \in [T_{\min},T_{\max}]$, $T_{\min}>0$.
\end{description}




\subsubsection{Inner Loop: Attitude Stabilization}
\label{sec:inner_loop}



With $e_\phi = \phi_{\mathrm{ref}} - \phi$ and the PD law
$\tau_\phi = K_p e_\phi + K_d \dot{e}_\phi$, (Assumption 1) and (Assumption 2) reduce the roll channel to

\begin{equation}
  I_{xx}\ddot{e}_\phi + (K_d+d_x)\dot{e}_\phi + K_p e_\phi = d_\phi(t),
  \qquad
  d_\phi = I_{xx}\ddot{\phi}_{\mathrm{ref}} + d_x\dot{\phi}_{\mathrm{ref}} - \Delta_\phi,
  \label{eq:err_roll}
\end{equation}

with $\Delta_\phi = -[\boldsymbol{\omega}_B \times J\boldsymbol{\omega}_B]_x$. In
state form $\mathbf{e}=[e_\phi,\dot{e}_\phi]^{\top}$,

\begin{equation}
  \dot{\mathbf{e}} = A\mathbf{e} + Bd_\phi, \qquad
  A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -K_p/I_{xx} & -(K_d+d_x)/I_{xx} \end{bmatrix},
  \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/I_{xx} \end{bmatrix}.
  \label{eq:err_state}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Inner-Loop LES]
\label{thm:inner}
$A$ is Hurwitz iff $K_p>0$ and $K_d+d_x>0$. For any positive defnite $Q$ the Lyapunov
equation $A^{\top}P+PA=-Q$ admits a unique positive definite $P$, and
$V(\mathbf{e})=\mathbf{e}^{\top}P\mathbf{e}$ certifies
\begin{equation}
  \|\mathbf{e}(t)\| \le \sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(P)/\lambda_{\min}(P)}\,
  \|\mathbf{e}(0)\|\,e^{-\alpha t}, \qquad
  \alpha = \lambda_{\min}(Q)/2\lambda_{\max}(P).
  \label{eq:les_bound}
\end{equation}

\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Routh--Hurwitz on $\lambda^2+\frac{K_d+d_x}{I_{xx}}\lambda+\frac{K_p}{I_{xx}}$
gives the stated condition. The converse Lyapunov theorem
\parencite{khalil_nonlinear_2002} yields $P$, \eqref{eq:les_bound}
follows from the comparison lemma \parencite{khalil_nonlinear_2002}. The
$\mathcal{O}(\|\cdot\|^2)$ residuals of (Assumption 1)--(Assumption 2) are dominated by
$\dot{V}\le-\lambda_{\min}(Q)\|\mathbf{e}\|^2$ in a neighbourhood of the
origin, so LES of the nominal linearisation persists for the true nonlinear
error dynamics \parencite{khalil_nonlinear_2002}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Inner-Loop ISS]
\label{cor:iss}
System \eqref{eq:err_state} is locally ISS with respect to $d_\phi$, with gain
\begin{equation}
  \gamma(s) = \sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(P)/\lambda_{\min}(P)}\,
  \frac{2\|PB\|}{\kappa\,\lambda_{\min}(Q)}\,s, \qquad \kappa\in(0,1).
  \label{eq:iss_gain}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
$V$ from Theorem~\ref{thm:inner} satisfies
$\dot V \le -(1-\kappa)\lambda_{\min}(Q)\|\mathbf{e}\|^2$ for
$\|\mathbf{e}\| \ge 2\|PB\|\,\|d_\phi\|_\infty/\kappa\lambda_{\min}(Q)$; this is
precisely an ISS-Lyapunov function \parencite{khalil_nonlinear_2002}.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Outer Loop: Kinematic Guidance Map}
\label{sec:outer_loop}

Write $f_{\mathrm{UHRC}}:\mathbb{R}^{45}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{D}$,
$(\mathbf{o}_t,\mathbf{z}_t)\mapsto[\phi_{\mathrm{ref}},\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}]^{\top}$,
$\mathcal{Z}=\mathbb{R}^{2\times C\times H}$, $\mathcal{D}=[-\pi/6,\pi/6]^2$.

\begin{lemma}[Hard Output Bound]
\label{lem:bounded}
$f_{\mathrm{UHRC}}(\mathbf{o},\mathbf{z})\in\mathcal{D}$ for all
$(\mathbf{o},\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{45}\times\mathcal{Z}$, $t\ge0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Immediate from $\mathrm{sat}_{\pi/6}(\cdot)$ applied element-wise at the action
head; holds independently of network weights or observations.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Local Lipschitz Regularity]
\label{lem:lipschitz}
On compact $\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{Z}_c$, $f_{\mathrm{UHRC}}$ is Lipschitz
with $L_{\mathrm{UHRC}} \le \prod_l \sigma_{\max}(W_l)\cdot\prod_k L_k^{\mathrm{act}} <\infty$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$f_{\mathrm{UHRC}}$ is a finite composition of: affine maps
($L=\sigma_{\max}(W)$); SiLU ($L\le1.1$); SwiGLU (product of the above);
scaled dot-product attention, Lipschitz on $\mathcal{O}$ since softmax is
1-Lipschitz and $QK^{\top}$ is bounded bilinear there; RMSNorm, Lipschitz for
$\|\mathbf{z}\|\ge\delta>0$; and $\mathrm{sat}_{\pi/6}$ (1-Lipschitz,
non-expansive). A finite composition of locally Lipschitz maps is locally
Lipschitz with constant bounded by the product of factors.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Cascade Interconnection: ISS and UUB}
\label{sec:cascade}

\begin{theorem}[Cascade UUB]
\label{thm:cascade_iss}
Under (Assumption 1)--(Assumption 4), Corollary~\ref{cor:iss}, and Lemma~\ref{lem:bounded}, the
rotational state $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{rot}}=(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\omega}_B)$
is uniformly ultimately bounded with ultimate bound
$\mu=\gamma(\pi/6)+\gamma(\ddot{\eta}_{\max}I_{xx}+\dot{\eta}_{\max}d_x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:bounded} the driving signal $\boldsymbol{\eta}_r$ lies in the
compact set $\mathcal{D}$; by (Assumption 3), $\|d_\phi\|_\infty<\infty$. An ISS system
(Corollary~\ref{cor:iss}) driven by a uniformly bounded input is UUB with bound
$\gamma(\|d_\phi\|_\infty)$ \parencite{khalil_nonlinear_2002}; no
small-gain condition is required since $\mathrm{sat}_{\pi/6}$ caps the
feedback path independently of the inner-loop state
\parencite{sontag_smooth_1989}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Timescale Separation Fails]
With $Q=I$, $\kappa=0.5$: $\|PB\|=0.588$, $\gamma(s)\approx 54.8\,s$. The EMA
reference filter ($\alpha=0.12$, 100\,Hz) has bandwidth
$\omega_{\mathrm{out}}\approx12.78$\,rad/s, exceeding the inner spectral
abscissa $|\varsigma(A)|=5.01$\,rad/s, i.e. $\epsilon=\omega_{\mathrm{out}}/|\varsigma(A)|\approx2.55\not\ll1$.
Singular perturbation reduction \parencite{khalil_nonlinear_2002} is
therefore not justified; the ISS cascade argument above requires no such
separation.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Translational Consequences and Convergence}
\label{sec:translation}

\begin{proposition}[Velocity UUB, Position Unbounded]
\label{prop:trans}
Under Theorem~\ref{thm:cascade_iss} and (Assumption 4),
$\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|\mathbf{v}_I^{\mathrm{h}}(t)\| \le T_{\max}\sin(\pi/6+\mu)/c_{\mathrm{lin}} <\infty$,
but $\|\mathbf{r}_I(t)-\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{goal}}\|$ admits no finite bound.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
From \eqref{eq:trans}, horizontal acceleration is bounded by
$T_{\max}\sin(\pi/6+\mu)/m$ and opposed by linear drag $c_{\mathrm{lin}}\mathbf{v}_I$;
the resulting scalar ODE $\dot v \le \bar{a}-\frac{c_{\mathrm{lin}}}{m}v$ has UUB
solutions with the stated bound. Since $\mathbf{v}_I^{\mathrm h}$ may settle at
any nonzero value within this bound (attitude UUB does not force
$\boldsymbol{\eta}\to\mathbf{0}$, only $\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathcal{B}_\mu$), a
persistent nonzero terminal tilt yields $\|\mathbf{r}_I(t)-\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{goal}}\|\to\infty$
linearly in $t$ whenever the terminal heading is not goal-directed.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Conditional Asymptotic Goal Convergence]
\label{thm:conv}
Let $V_{\mathrm{nav}}=\tfrac12\|\mathbf{r}_I-\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{goal}}\|^2$. If
$\exists\,\alpha_3\in\mathcal{K}$ such that
\begin{equation}
  \langle\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}V_{\mathrm{nav}},\,\mathbf{v}_I\rangle \le -\alpha_3(V_{\mathrm{nav}})
  \qquad\forall\,t\ge T
  \label{eq:gdc}
\end{equation}
outside $\mathcal{B}_{r_0}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{goal}})$, then
$\mathbf{r}_I(t)\to\mathcal{B}_{r_0}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{goal}})$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
\eqref{eq:gdc} is a property of the trained policy, not analytically
verifiable for $f_{\mathrm{UHRC}}$; behavioural cloning enforces it nowhere by
construction. Empirically, \eqref{eq:gdc} held throughout all successful
trials ($N=30$ per scenario) and was violated prior to onset of divergence in
all failures, consistent with Proposition~\ref{prop:trans}, which predicts
exactly this failure mode (bounded attitude, unbounded position) rather than
attitude instability or tumbling.
\end{remark}

\subsection{TC01: Open-Field Transit}
\label{sec:tc01}


TC01 establishes baseline goal-seeking performance in the absence of any obstacles.
The classical controller achieved 100\% success across all 30 trials, with a mean
RMSE of 0.938\,m and an IAE of 43.426\,m\,s. The residual tracking error is
attributable to cumulative drift in the ICP odometry subsystem and the fixed-interval
pose graph optimisation cycle: the INSEKF position estimator exhibited residual errors
of 0.376\,m along $x$ and 0.265\,m along $y$ under square-trajectory validation,
confirming that localisation noise propagates directly into the tracking error. The
PLR of 2.090 reflects the pure-pursuit tracker's lookahead-induced lateral oscillations
around the nominal straight-line path.

The UHRC-PID achieved a success rate of 30\% (9 of 30 trials) with a mean RMSE of
5.307\,m and a PLR of 2.09. The two successful
trials shared a common characteristic, the initial goal distance was below
approximately 7\,m, placing the goal within a range at which the learned policy
converged to a stable goal-seeking attractor before the carry state had drifted
out of distribution. On longer-range goals, prediction errors in the generated torques
compound across successive timesteps, driving the vehicle into states the model has
never encountered. Qualitative trajectories for both

\subsection{TC04: Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance}
\label{sec:tc04}


TC04 reveals the most significant result of the evaluation. Obstacles
are hidden from both controllers' sensor streams until $t \geq 3.0$\,s, at which
point they are revealed within the drone's active flight. The classical
controller recorded a 0\% success rate across all 30 trials, as
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tc04}.

The classical controller's failure is structural, obstacle detection is contingent on
the LiDAR scan being projected into the occupancy grid and a revised waypoint sequence
being computed by A* and propagated to the pure-pursuit tracker. The pipeline
introduces a latency between obstacle emergence and the availability of an updated
reference trajectory. During this window the low-level PID continues executing the
prior trajectory, and the collision occurs before replanning can redirect the vehicle,
as the trajectory in Figure~\ref{fig:tc04_classical} confirms. This latency is
inherent to the fixed supervisory-regulatory interface and is not addressable by
tuning within the existing architecture.

In trials where the UHRC-PID fails, is attributable to the policy instability that dominates all
test cases: the drone diverges from the nominal path before reaching the obstacle
rather than colliding with it. Minor erratic
atittitude predictions arise immediately after carry initialisation, consistent with the
policy encountering out-of-distribution states that compound before the obstacle is
ever revealed at $t = 3.0$\,s. Crucially, however, in the trials where the UHRC
remained on a coherent flight path until $t = 3.0$\,s , as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tc04_uhrc-pid}, the 32-ray LiDAR scan
immediately reflected the newly revealed obstacle returns, and the observation
embedding was updated within the same H-L reasoning cycle. This architecture-level
reactivity replanning latency bounded by a single 10\,ms inference call rather
than a 100\,ms pipeline cycle represents the key structural advantage of unified
reasoning over explicit hierarchical decomposition for dynamic scenarios.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results across TC01--TC05 reveal a consisten
picture. The classical controller's failures remain architecturally deterministic: the 
supervisory-regulatory interface introduces a replanning latency that is structurally 
irreducible without redesigning the pipeline. The UHRC-PID, conversely, demonstrates 
superior reactive spatial reasoning in dynamic environments, leveraging the inner-loop PID for rigid-body 
stabilization while the outer-loop neural network handles kinematic guidance. The two 
architectures therefore fail and succeed in qualitatively different ways that reveal 
complementary design boundaries.

\subsection{Policy Stability and Guidance Efficacy}
\label{sec:discuss_stability}

The implementation of the cascaded architecture serves as a proof of principle, that the UHRC can 
achieve highly effective goal-directed navigation under nominal conditions. By delegating 
high-frequency attitude regulation to the inner-loop PID controller, the outer-loop is freed from the burden of compensating for fast rotational dynamics, thereby 
mitigating the distributional compounding problem \parencite{le_mero_survey_2022, block_is_2024}. 

The integral correction mechanism of the classical inner loop provides a continuous 
restoring force against transient deviations. Consequently, in a representative open-field 
transit (TC01), the hybrid architecture achieved a Path Length Ratio (PLR) of 1.010, an 
Integral Absolute Error (IAE) of 37.092\,m$\cdot$s, and a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 
8.362\,m. These metrics confirm that when the episode is protected from unbounded attitude 
divergence, the learned spatial reasoning is functionally correct. The 
conclusion is that the spatial planning component of the UHRC has potential to be stable for 
long-horizon closed-loop operation once decoupled from low-level action generation.



\subsection{Overcoming the Information Bottleneck: TC02 and TC03}
\label{sec:discuss_bottleneck}

The theoretical motivation for the UHRC is resolving the information bottleneck at the
classical controller's supervisory-regulatory interface: the A* planner transmits
only a waypoint sequence to the low-level PID, discarding all contextual
information about obstacle proximity and local geometry \parencite{kolchinsky_nonlinear_2019,
abdelmaksoud_control_2020}. The TC02 and TC03 results provide strong empirical
evidence for this hypothesis. The classical controller exhibits lateral oscillation 
around the planned path near obstacle surfaces, attributable to the pure-pursuit tracker 
consuming only positional waypoints and having no representation of adjacent obstacle clearance. 

The UHRC-PID's trajectories in constrained scenarios show qualitatively smoother clearance 
geometry, consistent with the unified observation vector providing the L-module with direct 
access to LiDAR returns during attitude intent computation. For example, during symmetric corridor
transit (TC02), the UHRC-PID achieved a PLR of 1.081, although from observation, the flight path was suboptimal,
as compared to the classical controller. Similarly, during gap navigation (TC03), the model recorded a PLR of 1.171 and 
an RMSE of 6.455\,m. Because basic policy stability is maintained by the inner loop, the UHRC 
successfully demonstrates its architectural advantage in constrained geometric environments. 

\subsection{Architectural Highlights in Dynamic Environments: TC04}
\label{sec:discuss_tc04}

TC04 reveals the most architecturally significant result of the evaluation, showcasing 
where the learned hierarchy explicitly outperforms classical explicit decomposition. 
Obstacles are hidden from both controllers' sensor streams until $t \geq 3.0$\,s, at which
point they are revealed within the drone's active flight path. 

The classical controller's failure is displayed in obstacle detection being contingent on the 
LiDAR scan being projected into the occupancy grid and a revised waypoint sequence being 
computed by A* and propagated to the pure-pursuit tracker. This latency window ensures 
collision before replanning can redirect the vehicle \parencite{li_hierarchical_2023, 
zhu_hierarchical_2024}. This latency is inherent to the fixed supervisory-regulatory 
interface and is not addressable by tuning.

Conversely, the UHRC-PID successfully navigated the dynamic environment. A representative 
successful trial yielded a PLR of 3.104, an IAE of 178.014\,m$\cdot$s, and an RMSE of 9.001\,m. 
The LiDAR observation was updated within the same 10\,ms inference cycle as the obstacle 
revelation, and the observation embedding reflected the new obstacle geometry immediately in 
the next H-L reasoning cycle. This architecture-level reactivity—replanning latency bounded 
by a single inference call. This represents the key structural 
advantage of unified reasoning over explicit hierarchical decomposition for dynamic scenarios.

\subsection{Distributional Fragility vs Integral Robustness: TC05}
\label{sec:discuss_robustness}

TC05 most directly characterises the fundamental trade-off between the two
architectures under simultaneous perturbations of mass ($\pm 20\%$), inertia ($\pm 30\%$), 
drag ($\pm 50\%$), and thrust coefficient ($\pm 15\%$). 

The classical controller's success demonstrates that its cascaded integral feedback is a 
principled and highly effective mechanism for parametric robustness. Deviations in effective 
plant dynamics are treated as persistent disturbances by the PID integrators and compensated 
continuously, without any requirement for the controller to have knowledge of the perturbation 
magnitude.

The UHRC-PID demonstrates similar performance, though it still reflects a degree of distributional fragility. 
Behavioural cloning on a single nominal dynamic model produces a policy whose implicit representation of spatial 
kinematics is calibrated exclusively to the nominal parameter set \parencite{le_mero_survey_2022}. 
Analysis of the control inputs under perturbed conditions reveals elevated thrust commands 
(maintaining approximately 11.8\,N instead of the nominal 9.81\,N hover thrust), which is expected. As for the attitude commands, although a bounded contraint was enforced for trainig the UHRC, the model still produced  
high-frequency torque oscillations accounted for by prediction errors in attidtude references. The PID loop compensates for these errors, but the overall trajectory is still suboptimal. 

Despite these uncertainties, a successful perturbed transit achieved a PLR of 1.066, an IAE of 
63.861\,m$\cdot$s, and an RMSE of 4.391\,m. This demonstrates that grounding the execution in 
classical PID logic successfully anchors the degraded kinematic guidance of the HRM, establishing 
a significantly wider stability margin even when the outer-loop predictions degrade.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented the Unified Hierarchical Reasoning Controller (UHRC), the
first systematic application of the Hierarchical Reasoning Model (HRM) architecture
to a continuous nonlinear closed-loop control system. The model was ultimately formulated 
as a cascaded UHRC-PID hybrid, where a recurrent transformer outer-loop generates attitude 
intent, and a classical inner-loop handles high-frequency rigid-body stabilization. 
The comparative evaluation against a classical SLAM-augmented cascaded PID baseline
across five structured test cases produced principal findings. First, the HRM architecture 
can learn a highly functional continuous control policy when timescale separation is 
respected. Second, the cascaded UHRC-PID architecture successfully addresses the compounding 
error limitations that act as the primary obstacle to reliable deployment in monolithic 
end-to-end networks. Third, the unified architecture explicitly outperforms the classical 
baseline in dynamic environments (TC04), leveraging single-step inference reactivity to 
bypass structural replanning latencies. Finally, while the hybrid model improves general 
robustness, classical integral feedback remains the superior paradigm for rejecting 
severe, unmodelled parametric uncertainty.

The primary direction for future work is domain randomisation during training,
which directly addresses residual distributional fragility without architectural modification. Use of an ideal controller, in replace of the PID regulators to further improve the accurancy of the learned mode.
Finally, reinforcement learning fine-tuning from the behaviourally cloned initialisation
would further allow the policy to discover corrective strategies absent from the offline 
expert. Beyond these immediate extensions, the UHRC framework provides a principled foundation 
for investigating whether unified hierarchical reasoning can serve as the definitive 
outer-loop guidance architecture for complex autonomous systems.
