\begin{abstract}
This paper presents the Unified Hierarchical Reasoning Controller (UHRC), a novel framework designed to enhance the reasoning capabilities of 
artificial intelligence systems. The UHRC integrates hierarchical reasoning processes, allowing for more efficient and effective decision-making in complex environments. 
The results indicate that the UHRC can significantly improve the adaptability and robustness of AI systems, making it a promising approach for future research in artificial intelligence and machine learning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The autonomous control of complex nonlinear systems remains one of the most
consequential open problems in control engineering
\parencite{iqbal_nonlinear_2017, liu_new_2015, daoutidis_future_2023}.
Systems such as unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), autonomous ground vehicles (UGVs),
and high degree-of-freedom robotic manipulators are characterised by
high dimensional state spaces, nonlinear inter-state couplings, actuator
saturation, and significant parametric uncertainty
\parencite{sadati_two-level_2015, ameli_robust_2021, iqbal_nonlinear_2017,
slotine_applied_1991}.
The fundamental difficulties are that the dynamical regimes governing long horizon
navigation and those governing fast stabilisation operate on incommensurable
timescales, differ in their sensitivity to model error, and impose competing
demands on the same actuator plant
\parencite{liang_nonlinear_2018, abdelmaksoud_control_2020}.
Designing a controller that simultaneously addresses long horizon planning and
fast reactive stabilisation across these coupled dynamics is fundamentally a
multiscale reasoning problem, and no single architecture has proven universally
adequate across the competing demands of stability, adaptability, and
computational tractability \parencite{rojas_complex_2025, goodwin_control_2001}.
 
Classical hierarchical control has long addressed this multi-scale challenge
through explicit structural decomposition, a high-level supervisory layer
generates strategic plans and setpoints while a low-level regulatory layer
executes fast stabilisation and trajectory tracking
\parencite{singh_systems_1978, sadati_two-level_2015, scattolini_hierarchical_2007,
mesarovic_two_1970, wilson_foundations_1979}.
The theoretical underpinnings of this separation trace to the multilevel systems
framework of \parencite{mesarovic_two_1970} and the hierarchical decomposition
methods formalised in \parencite{singh_systems_1978, siljak_decentralized_2013},
which established that large scale optimisation problems can be rendered
tractable by decomposing them into coordinated subproblems across levels.
This architecture has achieved sustained success across aerospace, process
control, and robotics, benefiting from well established stability theory,
modular implementation, and interpretable failure modes
\parencite{sanders_hierarchical_1998, wang_hierarchical_2024, liu_generalized_2016,
bahnasawi_decentralised_1990, roberts_optimal_2001}.
The quadrotor UAV exemplifies this paradigm directly, the natural timescale
separation between slow translational dynamics and fast rotational dynamics
motivates a cascaded structure in which outer loop position planners generate
attitude references consumed by inner-loop attitude regulators
\parencite{hoffmann_quadrotor_2007, liang_nonlinear_2018, bouabdallah_pid_2004,
nagaty_control_2013, abdelhay_modeling_2019}.
Model predictive control has provided a principled realisation of the
supervisory layer in such architectures, enabling constraint aware planning
with formal stability guarantees at the coordination level
\parencite{scattolini_hierarchical_2007, wang_hierarchical_2024,
torrente_data-driven_2021}.
 
However, the fixed interface between layers introduces a structural information
bottleneck that becomes consequential as task complexity grows
\parencite{kolchinsky_nonlinear_2019, li_hierarchical_2023,
abdelmaksoud_control_2020}.
The high-level planner typically transmits heavily compressed information
ordinarily a kinematic waypoint sequence to the low-level regulator,
discarding contextual information regarding obstacle proximity, actuator
feasibility, and local geometric constraints that may be critical for downstream
control quality \parencite{zhu_hierarchical_2024, zhou_two-level_2017,
liu_adaptive_2013}.
Conversely, the low-level regulator executes reference trajectories without
access to the global context that motivated them, rendering local disturbance
responses globally suboptimal
\parencite{nguyen_identifying_2021, daoutidis_decomposition_2019}.
Fixed rule based supervisory frameworks also become brittle when the system
encounters scenarios not anticipated at design time
\parencite{sadati_hierarchical_2008, dave_design_2023}.
These limitations are not incidental but structural, they arise from the
requirement that the planner and regulator communicate through a
prespecified, fixed dimensional interface whose information content is
determined at design time rather than adapted online to task demands
\parencite{kolchinsky_nonlinear_2019, zhang_multi-level_2015}.
 
Learning based approaches have emerged as a principled alternative.
End-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) enables joint optimisation of planning
and regulation without manual interface specification
\parencite{almahamid_reinforcement_2021, levine_end--end_2016, kiran_deep_2022,
shakya_reinforcement_2023}.
Behavioural cloning provides a supervised alternative that sidesteps the
credit assignment challenges of RL by learning directly from expert
demonstrations, and has seen substantial success in autonomous driving and
manipulation \parencite{levine_end--end_2016, le_mero_survey_2022, ly_learning_2021,
block_is_2024}.
However, flat end-to-end policies are notoriously sample inefficient,
sensitive to reward shaping, and do not naturally maintain the multiscale
temporal structure that long horizon UAV navigation demands, the policy
allocates identical computational depth regardless of whether the task requires
strategic deliberation or reactive stabilisation
\parencite{almahamid_reinforcement_2021, agyei_deep_2025}.
Deep reinforcement learning approaches applied directly to quadrotor control
have demonstrated impressive agility in simulation
\parencite{trad_real-time_2024, saviolo_learning_2023}, but the sim-to-real gap,
sample complexity, and absence of formal stability guarantees remain open
challenges \parencite{dai_lyapunov-stable_2021, agyei_deep_2025}.
Hybrid neurosymbolic and fuzzy integrated controllers partially address this
brittleness by pairing learned components with structured decomposition
\parencite{tang_fuzzy_2024, mahmoud_fractional-order_2024, tran_hybrid_2021,
baheri_hierarchical_2025}, yet the supervisory-regulatory interface and its
dimensionality remain manually prescribed.
The boundary between planning and regulation is fixed by the designer, not
learned from data \parencite{borri_design_2019, yu_survey_2023}.
 
Recent advances in neural reasoning architectures offer a substantially
different approach to this problem.
Test time scaling methods that iterate computations in latent space
\parencite{geiping_scaling_2025, li_seek_2025} and recurrent architectures that
adapt computational depth to task difficulty
\parencite{geiping_scaling_2025, li_system_2025}
suggest that the depth of processing required for a given decision can be made
emergent rather than fixed.
The Hierarchical Reasoning Model (HRM), introduced by
\parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025}, is a two tier recurrent architecture comprising
a high-level module responsible for slow, abstract planning and a low-level
module handling rapid, detailed computation, coupled through a shared carry
state that enables adaptive allocation of computational depth.
Trained on as few as 1{,}000 examples without pre-training or chain-of-thought
supervision, the HRM achieves near perfect accuracy on complex Sudoku problems
and optimal pathfinding in large maze tasks on which state-of-the-art large
language model approaches fail entirely \parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025,
huang_towards_2023, feng_efficient_2025}.
Crucially, the hierarchical role specialisation between modules emerges through
training rather than being architecturally prescribed, mirroring the
representational dimensionality hierarchy observed across cortical regions in
the mammalian brain \parencite{badre_frontal_2018, huntenburg_large-scale_2018,
murray_hierarchy_2014}, where higher order areas operate over longer intrinsic
timescales and higher dimensional representations than primary sensory regions.
Building on this, \parencite{jin_decoupled_2025} demonstrated that decoupling
planning from execution in a hierarchical framework yields measurable gains in
multistep reasoning tasks, further motivating the application of this design
principle to closed loop control.
The hierarchical neuro-symbolic decision transformer of
\parencite{baheri_hierarchical_2025} similarly demonstrates that coupling a symbolic
planner with a learned low-level policy through a structured interface
outperforms both purely symbolic and purely neural baselines on sequential
decision tasks, providing additional evidence that the hierarchical
planning-execution separation confers a genuine computational advantage beyond
what either component achieves in isolation.
 
Nevertheless, all demonstrated HRM applications to date have been confined to
discrete, turn based domains.
Whether the same unified reasoning mechanism can function as a closed loop
controller for continuous, real time nonlinear dynamical systems where
control outputs must be physically continuous, observations arrive as dense
sensory streams, and temporal context must persist across thousands of control
cycles remains entirely unexplored in the literature.
Extending HRMs to such settings requires nontrivial architectural adaptations, discrete
token outputs must be replaced by continuous commands, and the supervised
training signal must be derived from a physically meaningful expert policy
rather than symbolic ground truth labels.
Localisation and mapping, which are prerequisite for any meaningful planning
computation, must be supplied by an onboard sensor
\parencite{gupta_simultaneous_2022, milijas_comparison_2021,
munguia_uav_2025, chen_slam_2022}
whose outputs form part of the continuous observation stream consumed by
the controller.
 
This paper addresses that gap by proposing and evaluating the
\textit{Unified Hierarchical Reasoning Controller} (UHRC) for the autonomous
navigation of a six-degree-of-freedom quadrotor UAV, constituting the first
systematic application of the HRM architecture to a continuous nonlinear
closed loop control system.
The UHRC adapts the HRM's two-level reasoning structure into a recurrent
transformer controller trained via behavioural cloning
\parencite{levine_end--end_2016, le_mero_survey_2022, block_is_2024}
on expert trajectory data generated by an A* path planner
\parencite{lavalle_planning_2006, qin_review_2023}
across 500 simulated episodes.
The architecture is evaluated against a classical hierarchical baseline
incorporating SLAM-based localisation \parencite{gupta_simultaneous_2022,
milijas_comparison_2021}, A* path planning, and cascaded PID control
\parencite{bouabdallah_pid_2004, ren_cascade_2016, abdelhay_modeling_2019,
lopez-sanchez_pid_2023}
across five structured test cases of increasing environmental complexity.

The primary contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The first empirical demonstration of an HRM-based architecture operating as a
          closed loop controller for a continuous nonlinear dynamic system, establishing
          the functional feasibility of unified hierarchical reasoning in the control domain.

    \item A UHRC architecture incorporating dual-stream fusion of kinematic and LiDAR
          observations, a rolling carry buffer for persistent temporal context, Rotary
          Positional Encoding (RoPE) over the carry sequence, and a gated subgoal injection
          mechanism coupling H-module planning to L-module computation.

    \item A behavioural cloning training pipeline employing a versatile episode distribution,
          A*-generated expert demonstrations with injected perturbations and a
          structured loss combining weighted action imitation, subgoal supervision,
          and alignment.

    \item A rigorous comparative empirical evaluation quantifying the performance robustness
          trade off between unified learned hierarchy and classical explicit decomposition
          across open field transit, constrained corridor navigation, gap threading, dynamic
          obstacle avoidance, and parametric model uncertainty.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

This section details the UHRC architecture, the classical hierarchical baseline against
which it is evaluated, the expert data generation procedure, the behavioural cloning
training pipeline, and the comparative evaluation protocol. All controllers share an
identical 6-DOF quadrotor plant, described first.


%
\subsection{Quadrotor Dynamics Model}
\label{sec:dynamics}

The benchmark plant is a rigid-body quadrotor modelled in the East-North-Up (ENU)
inertial frame $\mathcal{I}$ with body frame $\mathcal{B}$ fixed to the vehicle centre
of mass. The state vector is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x} = \bigl[\mathbf{r}_I^{\top},\;
                        \mathbf{v}_I^{\top},\;
                        \mathbf{q}_{BI}^{\top},\;
                        \boldsymbol{\omega}_B^{\top},\;
                        \boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top} \bigr]^{\top}
    \in \mathbb{R}^{17},
    \label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{r}_I \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and $\mathbf{v}_I \in \mathbb{R}^3$ are the
inertial position and velocity, $\mathbf{q}_{BI} \in \mathbb{R}^4$ is the unit quaternion
representing the rotation from $\mathcal{I}$ to $\mathcal{B}$ satisfying $\|\mathbf{q}_{BI}\| = 1$, $\boldsymbol{\omega}_B
\in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the angular velocity expressed in $\mathcal{B}$, and
$\boldsymbol{\Omega} \in \mathbb{R}^4$ contains the four rotor speeds.

The control input is the body-frame wrench
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{u} = \bigl[T,\; \tau_x,\; \tau_y,\; \tau_z\bigr]^{\top}
    \in \mathbb{R}^4,
\end{equation}
comprising the net collective thrust $T$ along the body $+z$ axis and roll, pitch, and
yaw torques $\tau_x$, $\tau_y$, $\tau_z$ respectively. This formulation bypasses
explicit rotor speed dynamics and assumes ideal wrench actuation, which is appropriate
for the simulation study conducted here \parencite{hoffmann_quadrotor_2007}.

The translational dynamics are governed by Newton's second law in the inertial frame:
\begin{equation}
    m\dot{\mathbf{v}}_I =
        mg +
        R_{IB}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ T\end{bmatrix} +
        C_d^{\text{lin}}\,\mathbf{v}_I,
    \label{eq:trans}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the vehicle mass, $g$ is gravitational acceleration,
$R_{IB} = R_{BI}^{\top}$ is the rotation matrix from $\mathcal{B}$ to $\mathcal{I}$
derived from $\mathbf{q}_{BI}$, and $C_d^{\text{lin}} = \mathrm{diag}(c_x, c_y, c_z)$
is the linear aerodynamic drag matrix. The rotational dynamics follow the
Newton-Euler equations in the body frame:
\begin{equation}
    J\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_B =
        \boldsymbol{\tau}_B -
        \boldsymbol{\omega}_B \times J\boldsymbol{\omega}_B -
        C_d^{\text{rot}}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_B,
    \label{eq:rot}
\end{equation}
where $J = \mathrm{diag}(I_{xx}, I_{yy}, I_{zz})$ is the diagonal inertia matrix,
$\boldsymbol{\tau}_B = [\tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z]^{\top}$, and
$C_d^{\text{rot}} = \mathrm{diag}(d_x, d_y, d_z)$ is the rotational damping matrix.
Attitude kinematics are propagated via the unit quaternion differential equation
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\mathbf{q}}_{BI} =
        \tfrac{1}{2}\,(\mathbf{q}_{BI})\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_B,
\end{equation}
where $(\mathbf{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times3}$ is the standard quaternion kinematic
matrix \parencite{shastry_development_2018}. The system is integrated using a
fourth-order Runge-Kutta scheme at a fixed timestep of $\Delta t = 0.01$\,s, with
quaternion renormalisation applied after each step. Physical parameters are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:quad_params}.

\subsection{Classical Hierarchical Baseline}
\label{sec:baseline}


The baseline is a four-stage classical hierarchical controller implementing the full
supervisory-regulatory decomposition described in the literature
\parencite{sanders_hierarchical_1998, scattolini_hierarchical_2007}. It serves as a
representative instance of the architecture class the UHRC is designed to replace.

An Inertial Navigation System Extended Kalman Filter (INSEKF) fuses GPS position and
Attitude and Heading Reference System (AHRS) orientation estimates at 10\,Hz to
produce a pose estimate $[\hat{\mathbf{r}}_I, \hat{\mathbf{q}}_{BI}]$. LiDAR
odometry is provided by an Iterative Closest Point (ICP) registration pipeline
operating on 2D point cloud keyframes, accumulated into a pose graph optimised via
Levenberg-Marquardt at fixed intervals of 500 nodes \parencite{gupta_simultaneous_2022,
milijas_comparison_2021}.

A collision-free path is computed by the A* algorithm on a 2D occupancy grid
constructed by projecting LiDAR returns onto the ground plane at 2.0\,m cell
resolution. The grid spans the drone's estimated flight level within a height band
of $\pm 2.0$\,m. Path feasibility is guaranteed if a collision-free grid path exists
\parencite{zhou_review_2021}; otherwise the controller holds the previously valid path.


A pure pursuit tracker with a fixed lookahead distance of $L_d = 0.8$\,m translates
the planned waypoint sequence into position setpoints at 10\,Hz
\parencite{huang_path_2020}.


A cascaded PID structure realises the position setpoints. The outer position loop
maps horizontal position errors to desired roll and pitch references
$[\phi_d, \theta_d]$ via the standard small-angle coupling
$\theta_d = a_x^{\mathrm{des}} / g$, $\phi_d = -a_y^{\mathrm{des}} / g$.
The inner attitude loop generates body wrench commands from attitude and altitude
errors using derivative-filtered PID controllers on each axis
\parencite{ren_efficient_2025,bouabdallah_design_2007}.


\subsection{UHRC Architecture}
\label{sec:uhrc_arch}

The UHRC is a two-level recurrent transformer that adapts the HRM architecture
\parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025} to continuous closed-loop control. At each timestep,
a high-level module $H$ generates a 3-dimensional velocity subgoal encoding strategic
intent, and a low-level module $L$ translates that intent together with a rich sensory
context into a 4-dimensional body wrench command. Both modules maintain persistent
memory through a rolling carry buffer that replaces the HRM's per-problem hidden state,
enabling temporal reasoning across the full episode horizon.


\subsubsection{Observation Representation}
\label{sec:obs}

All observations are expressed in the body frame $\mathcal{B}$. This choice renders
the observation invariant to global heading and position, reducing the domain of the
mapping the controller must learn: an identical avoidance manoeuvre is appropriate
regardless of the vehicle's absolute world-frame orientation \cite{liang_nonlinear_2018}.
The 45-dimensional observation vector is

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{o} =
    \bigl[
        \underbrace{\mathbf{v}_B^{\top}}_{\times 3},\;
        \underbrace{\boldsymbol{\omega}_B^{\top}}_{\times 3},\;
        \underbrace{\mathbf{g}_B^{\top}}_{\times 3},\;
        \underbrace{\Delta\mathbf{r}_B^{\top}}_{\times 3},\;
        \underbrace{d_{\mathrm{norm}}}_{\times 1},\;
        \underbrace{\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{lidar}}^{\top}}_{\times 32}
    \bigr]^{\top}
    \in \mathbb{R}^{45},
    \label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}_B = R_{BI}\mathbf{v}_I$ is the body-frame translational velocity,
$\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$ is the angular velocity, $\mathbf{g}_B = R_{BI}[0, 0, -g]^{\top}$
encodes attitude implicitly by projecting the gravity vector into $\mathcal{B}$,
$\Delta\mathbf{r}_B = R_{BI}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{goal}} - \mathbf{r}_I)$ is the
goal displacement clipped to a maximum range $r_{\max}^{\mathrm{goal}} = 20$\,m,
$d_{\mathrm{norm}} = \min(\|\Delta\mathbf{r}_B\|, 20) / 20$ is the normalised
goal distance, and $\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{lidar}} \in [0, 1]^{32}$ is a 32-ray
planar LiDAR scan with field of view $\pi$\,rad and maximum range 5.0\,m,
normalised to the unit interval.

The separate scalar $d_{\mathrm{norm}}$ is included deliberately, without it,
the goal displacement vector $\Delta\mathbf{r}_B$ loses magnitude information as the
drone approaches the goal, causing the controller to overshoot. At runtime, the
observation is standardised by precomputed dataset statistics before being passed
to the model.


\subsubsection{Dual-Stream Input Encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}

The 13 scalar kinematic features $[\mathbf{v}_B^{\top}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_B^{\top},
\mathbf{g}_B^{\top}, \Delta\mathbf{r}_B^{\top}, d_{\mathrm{norm}}]$ and the 32-ray
LiDAR scan are processed by separate encoder branches before fusion.

A two-layer MLP first projects the 13-dimensional input to
$\mathbb{R}^{h/2}$ through a scaled hyperbolic tangent:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{s} = W_2 \tanh\!\bigl(\sqrt{H}\cdot W_1 \mathbf{o}_{\mathrm{kin}} + \mathbf{b}_1\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $h = 128$ is the model hidden dimension. The scaling factor $\sqrt{H}$
expands the effective dynamic range of kinematic inputs into the regime where $\tanh$
provides informative gradients, preventing early saturation during training.

The 32 normalised range returns are processed as a 1D signal
by a two-stage convolutional encoder:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{l} = W_{\mathrm{proj}}\,
        \mathrm{Flatten}\!\bigl(
            \sigma\!\bigl(\mathrm{Conv}_{k=3}^{16}(
                \sigma(\mathrm{Conv}_{k=5}^{16}(\mathbf{o}_{\mathrm{lidar}})))\bigr)
        \bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the SiLU activation, $\mathrm{Conv}_{k}^{c}$ denotes a
1D convolution with $c$ output channels and kernel size $k$ (same padding), and
$W_{\mathrm{proj}} \in \mathbb{R}^{(h/2) \times 512}$ projects the flattened
$16 \times 32 = 512$ feature map to $\mathbb{R}^{h/2}$.

The two $h/2$-dimensional branch outputs are concatenated and
linearly projected to a single embedding $\mathbf{e}_t = W_f[\mathbf{s}; \mathbf{l}]
\in \mathbb{R}^{h}$ representing the current observation at timestep $t$.


\subsubsection{Transformer Reasoning Blocks}
\label{sec:blocks}

Both the $H$-module and $L$-module are composed of stacked \texttt{UHRCBlock} layers,
each implementing a standard pre-normalised transformer encoder block:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{z}' &= \mathrm{RMSNorm}\bigl(\mathbf{z} + \mathrm{MHA}(\mathbf{z})\bigr),
    \label{eq:attn_block} \\
    \mathbf{z}'' &= \mathrm{RMSNorm}\bigl(\mathbf{z}' + \mathrm{SwiGLU}(\mathbf{z}')\bigr),
    \label{eq:mlp_block}
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{MHA}(\cdot)$ is non-causal multi-head self-attention with
$n_h = 4$ heads and head dimension $d_h = h / n_h = 32$, and $\mathrm{SwiGLU}(\cdot)$
is the gated feed-forward network
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SwiGLU}(\mathbf{z}) =
        W_{\downarrow}\bigl(\mathrm{SiLU}(W_g \mathbf{z}) \odot W_u \mathbf{z}\bigr),
\end{equation}
with intermediate dimension $d_{\mathrm{ff}} = 512$. Non-causal attention is appropriate here because the carry
represents a temporal history rather than an autoregressive prediction task, each
position can attend to all other positions, including more recent ones.

Rotary Positional Encoding (RoPE) is applied independently to the query and key
matrices within each attention head, encoding the relative age of each position in
the carry buffer. For a sequence of length $T$ and head dimension $d_h$, the
rotary frequencies are
\begin{equation}
    \theta_i = \frac{1}{10000^{2i/d_h}}, \quad i = 0, 1, \ldots, d_h/2 - 1,
\end{equation}
with cosine and sine tables precomputed up to the maximum sequence length of
$T_{\max} = \mathrm{carry\_len} + 1 = 17$. RoPE is preferred over learned absolute
positional embeddings because it generalises to sequence positions not seen during
training \parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025}.


\subsubsection{Persistent Temporal Carry Buffer}
\label{sec:carry}

In the original HRM, hidden states $\mathbf{z}_H$ and $\mathbf{z}_L$ are
re-initialised for each input problem. In closed-loop control, the controller must
maintain situational awareness across successive timesteps, tracking velocity history,
recognising developing obstacle geometries, and anticipating trajectory evolution.
The UHRC therefore treats the hidden states as persistent rolling buffers:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_{H}, \mathbf{z}_{L} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times h},
\end{equation}
where $B$ is the batch size (1 at inference), $C = 16$ is the carry length, and
$h = 128$ is the hidden dimension. At each timestep, the new observation embedding
$\mathbf{e}_t$ is appended to the buffer and the oldest entry is discarded:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{roll\_carry}(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{e}_t) =
        \bigl[\mathbf{z}_{:,1:C,:};\; \mathbf{e}_t^{\top}\bigr]
    \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times h},
\end{equation}
providing a fixed-length sliding window of temporal context without unbounded memory
growth. The carry therefore functions as a learned recurrent state that compresses
the episode's observational history into a fixed-size representation, structurally
analogous to the reasoning context in recurrent language models.

Carry tokens appended to the buffer are always gradient-detached (\texttt{DETACH\_CARRY=True}).
This truncates backpropagation through the carry boundary, preventing gradient chains
from extending beyond the training sequence window of length $T_{\mathrm{seq}} = 8$
steps. Empirical evaluation confirmed that this convention does not impair the quality
of learned temporal representations within the window, consistent with findings in
the HRM literature \parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025}. At the start of each episode, both
buffers are initialised to learned constant vectors $\mathbf{h}_H^{\mathrm{init}}$
and $\mathbf{h}_L^{\mathrm{init}}$ tiled to shape $[1, C, h]$.


\subsubsection{H-L Mutual Refinement and One-Step Gradient Approximation}
\label{sec:hl_cycles}

The $H$- and $L$-modules interact through $H_\mathrm{cyc} = 2$ outer cycles each
containing $L_\mathrm{cyc} = 2$ inner $L$-module passes. At each step, the full
sequence $\mathbf{z}_H^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 17 \times h}$ is formed by
concatenating the $C = 16$ carry tokens with the current observation embedding:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_H^{(t)} = \bigl[\mathbf{Z}_H;\; \mathbf{e}_t^{\top}\bigr], \quad
    \mathbf{z}_L^{(t)} = \bigl[\mathbf{Z}_L;\; \mathbf{e}_t^{\top}\bigr].
\end{equation}
An observation re-injection tensor
$\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{obs}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 17 \times h}$ is constructed
with zeros at carry positions and $\mathbf{e}_t$ at position 16, ensuring the raw
observation is available as an additive bias at every $L$-module call — a critical
structural correspondence to the HRM's input injection mechanism \parencite{wang_hierarchical_2025}.

To bound the gradient graph depth and maintain training stability, the UHRC adopts
the one-step gradient approximation from the HRM: all but the final module call in
the refinement cycle are computed under \texttt{torch.no\_grad()}, and only a single
$L$-pass followed by a single $H$-pass contribute to the gradient. With
$H_\mathrm{cyc} = 2$ and $L_\mathrm{cyc} = 2$, the execution schedule is:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
    \item \textbf{No-grad}: $L \to L \to H \to L$ \quad (3 $L$-calls, 1 $H$-call)
    \item \textbf{Gradient}: $L \to H$ \quad (1 $L$-call, 1 $H$-call)
\end{enumerate}
The effective gradient path traverses $2 + 2 = 4$ transformer layers in total,
roughly 3.5$\times$ shallower than the full 14-layer computational graph, preventing
gradient vanishing while retaining the representational benefit of multiple refinement
rounds. The $L$-module update at each call is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_L \leftarrow \mathcal{L}\bigl(\mathbf{z}_L + \mathbf{z}_H + \mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{obs}}\bigr),
\end{equation}
and the $H$-module update is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_H \leftarrow \mathcal{H}(\mathbf{z}_H + \mathbf{z}_L),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}(\cdot)$ and $\mathcal{H}(\cdot)$ denote the respective stacks of
\texttt{UHRCBlock} layers.


\subsubsection{Subgoal Generation, Gated Injection, and Action Output}
\label{sec:subgoal}

Following the final $H$-module update, the terminal hidden state $\mathbf{z}_H[:,16,:]
\in \mathbb{R}^{B \times h}$ (the most recent position in the carry sequence) is
decoded by a two-layer MLP planning head into a 3-dimensional body-frame velocity
subgoal:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}} = W_2\,\mathrm{SiLU}(W_1 \mathbf{z}_H[:,16,:])
    \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 3}.
\end{equation}
This subgoal encodes the H-module's strategic intent as a desired velocity vector
$[v_x, v_y, v_z]$ in the body frame, providing a physically interpretable interface
between the two modules.

The subgoal is injected into the $L$-module through a gated additive mechanism
designed to modulate the reactive representation without overriding it:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{g} &= \sigma\bigl(W_g \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}} + \mathbf{b}_g\bigr)
        \in (0,1)^{B \times h}, \label{eq:gate} \\
    \mathbf{c} &= \bigl(W_p \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}}\bigr) \odot \mathbf{g}
        \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times h}, \label{eq:context}
\end{align}
where $\sigma$ is the element-wise sigmoid. The gate bias is initialised to
$\mathbf{b}_g = +2.0$, so that $\sigma(+2) \approx 0.88$ at the start of training
keeping the gate mostly open and allowing the subgoal signal to propagate freely
before the planning head has converged. The gated context is broadcast across the
carry sequence and the $L$-module performs a final two-layer attention pass:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_L^{\mathrm{final}} =
        \mathcal{L}\bigl(\mathbf{z}_L + \mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{17}^{\top}\bigr),
\end{equation}
from which the body-frame wrench action is decoded from the terminal position:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{u}} = W_a\,\mathbf{z}_L^{\mathrm{final}}[:,16,:]
        = [\hat{F}_z,\; \hat{\tau}_x,\; \hat{\tau}_y,\; \hat{\tau}_z]^{\top}
        \in \mathbb{R}^{4}.
\end{equation}
The action head weight is initialised with standard deviation $10^{-2}$ and the
thrust bias is set to $\hat{F}_z^{(0)} = 9.81$\,N (hover thrust for a 1\,kg vehicle),
ensuring the network produces a stable hover at initialisation before any learning
has occurred. The full architectural configuration is summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:arch_config}.

\subsection{Expert Data Generation}
\label{sec:datagen}


Training data are generated by rolling out an expert policy comprising an A* path
planner, a pure-pursuit tracker, and the cascaded PID attitude controller on 500
successful simulated episodes at $\Delta t = 0.01$\,s per step
\parencite{levine_end--end_2016, le_mero_survey_2022}.


At the start of each episode, a 2D occupancy grid is constructed at a resolution of
$r_{\mathrm{grid}} = 0.25$\,m per cell, spanning the convex hull of the start
position, goal position, and all obstacle extents with a 2.0\,m margin. Circular
obstacles are inflated by $r_{\mathrm{inf}} = 0.25$\,m to account for vehicle body
radius. The A* algorithm searches an 8-connected neighbourhood using the octile
heuristic, guaranteeing the shortest collision-free path if one exists
\parencite{zhou_review_2021}. Episodes for which no path exists are discarded before
simulation begins.


The planned path is smoothed by three passes of a cubic averaging filter and tracked
by a pure-pursuit controller with lookahead $L_d = 1.0$\,m and maximum speed
$V_{\max} = 2.0$\,m/s. Speed is linearly ramped down to $0.3$\,m/s within 2\,m of
the goal. Upon entering a $0.1$\,m goal acceptance radius, the tracker issues a zero
velocity command for 150 additional steps (1.5\,s), demonstrating the hover
termination behaviour to the model.


To promote policy generalisation beyond the default $+x$-direction transit, six
episode categories are sampled according to the distribution in
Table~\ref{tab:episode_types}.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering

\label{tab:episode_types}
\begin{tabular}{lll}

\hline

The omnidirectional episodes are critical for eliminating the directional bias
intrinsic to the dominant $+x$-transit training distribution: without them, the
learned policy fails to navigate in the $-x$ or $\pm y$ directions.

Every 50 steps with probability 0.3, the current state receives a Gaussian velocity
perturbation ($\sigma = 0.2$\,m/s) and a small random yaw offset
($|\delta\psi| \leq 0.15$\,rad). This DAgger-style augmentation \parencite{levine_end--end_2016}
exposes the expert to a wider distribution of states and prevents the compounding
error pathology characteristic of pure behavioural cloning.


Episodes are included only if: (i) the drone reached the goal without collision;
(ii) the goal distance decreased monotonically over coarse 100-step windows; and
(iii) the average velocity subgoal magnitude during the final hover phase was below
$0.3$\,m/s. These criteria filter episodes in which the expert made insufficient
progress or failed to demonstrate a clean stopping behaviour.


\subsection{Behavioural Cloning Training}
\label{sec:training}


\subsubsection{Dataset Construction}

The \texttt{ControlDataset} constructs sequence windows of length
$T_{\mathrm{seq}} = 8$ timesteps from the stored episode data. Windows never cross
episode boundaries; windows that begin within the first $T_{\mathrm{seq}} - 1$ steps
of an episode are left-padded with zeros. The 45-dimensional observations are
$z$-scored using dataset-wide mean and standard deviation statistics. Actions and
velocity subgoals are retained in their raw physical units (N and m/s respectively)
so that the magnitude of control effort and velocity urgency are preserved as
supervisory signals. The dataset is split into training and validation subsets by
episode, with 10\% of episodes reserved for validation.

\subsubsection{Loss Function}

Three loss terms are combined:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}} +
                  \alpha_s \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sub}} +
                  \alpha_t \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{trk}},
    \label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha_s = 0.1$ and $\alpha_t = 0.5$.


The four action components $[F_z, \tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z]$ have very different
variances in the training data: thrust is tightly concentrated near the hover value
$F_z^{\mathrm{hover}} = 9.81$\,N, while the torque components span a much smaller
absolute range but carry proportionally more information per unit deviation. A
per-component weighted MSE is used to compensate:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}} =
        \Bigl\langle
            \bigl(\hat{\mathbf{u}} - \mathbf{u}^*\bigr)^{\!\top}
            \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{w}_a)
            \bigl(\hat{\mathbf{u}} - \mathbf{u}^*\bigr)
        \Bigr\rangle,
\end{equation}
where $\langle\cdot\rangle$ denotes the mean over the batch and $\mathbf{w}_a =
[0.68, 71.4, 69.0, 50.0] / \bar{w}_a$ are the normalised component weights.


An analogous weighted MSE on the 3-dimensional velocity subgoal:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sub}} =
        \Bigl\langle
            \bigl(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}} - \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{sub}}^*\bigr)^{\!\top}
            \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{w}_s)
            \bigl(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathrm{sub}} - \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{sub}}^*\bigr)
        \Bigr\rangle,
\end{equation}
with weights $\mathbf{w}_s = [1.5, 1.5, 0.1] / \bar{w}_s$. The vertical component
$v_z$ is down-weighted because episodes are conducted at constant altitude,
making the vertical subgoal largely uninformative.


The H-module subgoal and L-module torques must be physically consistent: pitch torque
$\tau_y$ produces forward acceleration that realises $v_x$, and roll torque $\tau_x$
produces lateral acceleration that realises $v_y$. The tracking loss penalises
misalignment between the horizontal subgoal direction $[v_x, v_y]^{\top}$ and the
corresponding torque vector $[\tau_y, \tau_x]^{\top}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{trk}} =
        \Bigl\langle
            \max\Bigl(0,
                -\frac{[\hat{v}_x,\hat{v}_y]}{\|[\hat{v}_x,\hat{v}_y]\| + \epsilon}
                 \cdot
                 \frac{[\hat{\tau}_y,\hat{\tau}_x]}{\|[\hat{\tau}_y,\hat{\tau}_x]\| + \epsilon}
            \Bigr)
        \Bigr\rangle.
    \label{eq:tracking_loss}
\end{equation}
This term acts as a soft architectural consistency constraint, encouraging the
inter-module coupling to reflect the correct body-frame dynamics.

\subsubsection{Optimiser and Schedule}

The model is trained with AdamW \cite{levine_end--end_2016} using the configuration
in Table~\ref{tab:train_config}. A linear warmup phase over the first 5 epochs raises
the learning rate from zero to its peak value, after which cosine annealing decays it
to approximately zero over the remaining 15 epochs. Gradients are clipped to a global
$\ell_2$ norm of 1.0 at each step. Automatic mixed precision (AMP) with a gradient
scaler is employed, reducing memory consumption to accommodate a batch size of 512.

\subsection{Comparative Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval_protocol}


Both controllers are evaluated across five structured test cases (TC01--TC05) of
increasing environmental complexity. To ensure a fair comparison, both controllers
receive ideal state feedback from the same underlying simulation state; neither
suffers from sensor noise or state estimation error. Start positions, goal positions,
and obstacle configurations are seeded identically for both controllers. Each
test case is repeated for $N = 30$ randomised Monte Carlo trials and metrics are
averaged over successful and failed trials.

\textbf{TC01 -- Open Transit.} Straight-line goal-seeking in an obstacle-free
environment, establishing baseline trajectory tracking performance.

\textbf{TC02 -- Symmetric Corridor.} Six obstacles arranged symmetrically around the
nominal flight path, testing multi-obstacle avoidance and path quality under spatial
constraint.

\textbf{TC03 -- Gap Navigation.} Two obstacles forming a 3\,m gap directly in the
nominal flight path, assessing control precision and gap-threading capability.

\textbf{TC04 -- Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance.} Obstacles are excluded from the
controller's sensor data until $t \geq 3.0$\,s, then revealed, requiring mid-flight
reactive replanning. Both controllers receive identical sensor data at each timestep.

\textbf{TC05 -- Parametric Robustness.} The TC02 obstacle configuration is retained
but the plant dynamics are perturbed: mass $\pm 20$\%, diagonal inertia $\pm 30$\%,
drag coefficients $\pm 50$\%, and thrust coefficient $k_T$ $\pm 15$\%. This
characterises each controller's robustness margin under distributional shift.

The primary performance metrics are root mean square error (RMSE) of the Euclidean
position error, integral absolute error (IAE), integral time-weighted absolute error
(ITAE), path length ratio (PLR = actual path length / straight-line distance), episode
success rate (goal reached within 0.5\,m without collision), and collision rate.

\subsection{TC01: Open-Field Transit}
\label{sec:tc01}


TC01 establishes baseline goal-seeking performance in the absence of any obstacles.
The classical controller achieved 100\% success across all 30 trials, with a mean
RMSE of 0.938\,m and an IAE of 43.426\,m\,s. The residual tracking error is
attributable to cumulative drift in the ICP odometry subsystem and the fixed-interval
pose graph optimisation cycle: the INSEKF position estimator exhibited residual errors
of 0.376\,m along $x$ and 0.265\,m along $y$ under square-trajectory validation,
confirming that localisation noise propagates directly into the tracking error. The
PLR of 2.090 reflects the pure-pursuit tracker's lookahead-induced lateral oscillations
around the nominal straight-line path.

The UHRC achieved a success rate of 6.7\% (2 of 30 trials) with a mean RMSE of
67.040\,m and a PLR of 18.74. The extremely high RMSE and PLR values reflect possibly that the policy diverges away from the goal rather than colliding
with obstacles, as confirmed by a timeout rate of 90\% of trials. The two successful
trials shared a common characteristic, the initial goal distance was below
approximately 7\,m, placing the goal within a range at which the learned policy
converged to a stable goal-seeking attractor before the carry state had drifted
out of distribution. On longer-range goals, prediction errors in the generated torques
compound across successive timesteps, driving the vehicle into states the model has
never encountered, and the absence of integral correction means no mechanism exists
to recover from these accumulated deviations. Qualitative trajectories for both
controllers are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:tc01_uhrc} and~\ref{fig:tc01_classical}.

\subsection{TC03: Gap Navigation}
\label{sec:tc03}

TC03 places two obstacles forming a 3\,m gap directly in the nominal flight path,
testing the precision of gap identification and the control accuracy required to pass
through without collision. The classical controller achieved 100\% success with a RMSE
of 0.631\,m and a PLR of 1.158. A* identified the gap and generated a path through it
in all trials; however, several trials exhibited near-miss events during gap transit,
attributable to waypoint discretisation at the 2.0\,m grid resolution and the
replanning latency at 10\,Hz, which together prevent precise path placement relative
to obstacle surfaces.

The UHRC achieved a success rate of 3.3\% with a mean RMSE of 48.413\,m and a PLR of
10.60. The PLR is marginally lower than in TC01 and TC02, consistent with the
observation that the presence of an obstacle structure near the flight path partially
constrains the divergence geometry compared to the open-field case. The trajectory in
Figure~\ref{fig:tc03_uhrc} shows that the UHRC's approach trajectory is smooth and
accurately directed toward the gap, indicating that the H-module's velocity subgoal
correctly encodes the gap as a navigable passage. The failure to complete the transit
is again attributable to torque prediction errors during and after gap transit, which
drive the vehicle into unstable attitude states.

\subsection{TC04: Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance}
\label{sec:tc04}


TC04 reveals the most architecturally significant result of the evaluation. Obstacles
are hidden from both controllers' sensor streams until $t \geq 3.0$\,s, at which
point they are revealed within the drone's active flight corridor. Both the classical
controller and the pure UHRC recorded a 0\% success rate across all 30 trials, as
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tc04}.

The classical controller's failure is structural, obstacle detection is contingent on
the LiDAR scan being projected into the occupancy grid and a revised waypoint sequence
being computed by A* and propagated to the pure-pursuit tracker. The pipeline
introduces a latency between obstacle emergence and the availability of an updated
reference trajectory. During this window the low-level PID continues executing the
prior trajectory, and the collision occurs before replanning can redirect the vehicle,
as the trajectory in Figure~\ref{fig:tc04_classical} confirms. This latency is
inherent to the fixed supervisory-regulatory interface and is not addressable by
tuning within the existing architecture.

The UHRC's failure is attributable to the same policy instability that dominates all
test cases: the drone diverges from the nominal path before reaching the obstacle
rather than colliding with it, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tc04_uhrc}. Large erratic
torque predictions arise immediately after carry initialisation, consistent with the
policy encountering out-of-distribution states that compound before the obstacle is
ever revealed at $t = 3.0$\,s. Crucially, however, in the trials where the UHRC
remained on a coherent flight path until $t = 3.0$\,s, the 32-ray LiDAR scan
immediately reflected the newly revealed obstacle returns, and the observation
embedding was updated within the same H-L reasoning cycle. This architecture-level
reactivity replanning latency bounded by a single 10\,ms inference call rather
than a 100\,ms pipeline cycle represents the key structural advantage of unified
reasoning over explicit hierarchical decomposition for dynamic scenarios, and
motivates the 20\% success rate observed in the UHRC-PID hybrid variant reported
separately.

\subsection{TC05: Parametric Robustness}
\label{sec:tc05}
TC05 applies simultaneous parametric perturbations to the plant dynamics (mass
$\pm 20\%$, diagonal inertia $\pm 30\%$, drag coefficients $\pm 50\%$, thrust
coefficient $k_T \pm 15\%$) over the TC02 corridor configuration. The two
controllers exhibit contrasting responses that directly characterise the
performance-robustness boundary between learned and classical architectures.

The classical controller achieved 100\% success across all 30 perturbed trials with
a mean RMSE of 0.707\,m, an IAE of 13.346\,m\,s, and a PLR of 1.227, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:tc05_classical}. These metrics are comparable to the nominal TC01 and
TC03 results, demonstrating bounded performance degradation under significant
parametric variation. The thrust output under perturbed conditions (2814.94\,Nm) was elevated
relative to the nominal TC01 baseline (5136.5\,Nm), consistent with the integral term
actively compensating for reduced effective hover thrust.

The UHRC achieved a success rate of 13.3\% (4 of 30 trials) under TC05 conditions,
with a mean RMSE of 58.675\,m and an ITAE of 73{,}011.560\,m\,s$^2$. A direct
comparison between the UHRC under nominal and perturbed conditions is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:tc05_uhrc_compare}: under nominal dynamics
(Figure~\ref{fig:tc05_uhrc_nominal}) the policy executes a coherent straight-line
transit, while under 20\% model uncertainty
(Figure~\ref{fig:tc05_torque_uncertainty}) the trajectory deviates substantially from the
intended path. The ITAE under TC05 is approximately $5\times$ larger than TC04,
because the parametric perturbations cause torque prediction errors that accumulate
and compound continuously over the full episode duration, producing large late-episode
deviations weighted heavily by the ITAE time penalty.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}


The results across TC01--TC05 collectively reveal a clear and internally consistent
picture. The UHRC's dominant failure mode is not a failure of spatial reasoning but
a failure of policy stability, the vehicle diverges from its intended path due to
compounding torque prediction errors, irrespective of whether obstacles are present.
This distinction is critical for interpreting the comparative results fairly. The
classical controller's failures, by contrast, are architecturally deterministic:
the supervisory-regulatory interface introduces a replanning latency that is
structurally irreducible without redesigning the pipeline. The two architectures
therefore fail in qualitatively different ways that reveal complementary design
boundaries rather than a simple performance ranking.


\subsection{Policy Instability as the Dominant Failure Mode}
\label{sec:discuss_instability}


Across TC01, TC02, and TC03, success rates of 6.7\%, 3.3\%, and 3.3\% respectively
confirm that the UHRC consistently fails to complete goal-directed navigation under
nominal conditions. The mean RMSE values of 67.040\,m, 45.476\,m, and 48.413\,m
are far in excess of the geometric scale of each task (start-to-goal distances of
approximately 10--18\,m), confirming that the dominant failure is trajectory
divergence rather than collision. This divergence originates from a
distributional compounding problem, the behavioural cloning training objective
minimises one-step prediction error, but at inference the model operates in a
closed loop where every prediction error perturbs the state, causing the next
observation to drift progressively further from the training distribution
\cite{le_mero_survey_2022, block_is_2024}.

The absence of an integral correction mechanism compounds this effect. The cascaded
PID controller accumulates tracking error in the integrator state of each loop,
providing a continuous restoring force that grows in proportion to the accumulated
deviation. The UHRC has no equivalent mechanism. The carry state encodes a
compressed representation of recent observations, but provides no signal that
distinguishes a large persistent error from a sequence of transient deviations.
Minor torque prediction errors therefore accumulate without bound until the vehicle
enters states the policy has never encountered. The two-layer transformer in the
L-module then operates in a regime where the input distribution has shifted
entirely outside the training manifold, and the resulting torque commands drive
further instability in a positive feedback loop.

The short-range successes in TC01 (2 of 30 trials with goal distances below
approximately 7\,m) confirm that the learned spatial reasoning is functionally
correct when the episode terminates before compounding errors dominate. This is
further supported by the qualitative trajectory evidence in TC02 and TC03, where
the UHRC's approach trajectories are smooth and geometrically appropriate the
H-module correctly identifies the corridor passage and the gap as navigable
subgoals but the vehicle fails to maintain stability long enough to execute
them. The conclusion is that the spatial planning component of the UHRC is
working as intended, but the action generation component lacks the error
correction properties needed for stable long-horizon closed-loop operation.


\subsection{Information Bottleneck vs Policy Stability: TC02 and TC03}
\label{sec:discuss_bottleneck}


The theoretical motivation for the UHRC is the information bottleneck at the
classical controller's supervisory-regulatory interface: the A* planner transmits
only a waypoint sequence to the low-level PID, discarding all contextual
information about obstacle proximity and local geometry \cite{kolchinsky_nonlinear_2019,
abdelmaksoud_control_2020}. The TC02 and TC03 results provide partial empirical
evidence for this hypothesis. In both cases the classical controller exhibits
lateral oscillation around the planned path near obstacle surfaces, attributable
to the pure-pursuit tracker consuming only positional waypoints and having no
representation of adjacent obstacle clearance. The UHRC's single successful
trajectories in these scenarios show qualitatively smoother clearance geometry,
consistent with the unified observation vector providing the L-module with direct
access to LiDAR returns during torque computation.

However, this architectural advantage is entirely negated by policy instability
in 97\% of trials. The information bottleneck hypothesis predicts that the UHRC
should outperform the classical controller in constrained geometric environments.
The results show instead that the UHRC cannot demonstrate this advantage because
it fails to remain stable long enough for the spatial reasoning benefit to
manifest. This does not invalidate the hypothesis, it establishes that
resolving policy instability is a necessary precondition for testing it
rigorously.


\subsection{Architectural Failure Modes in TC04}
\label{sec:discuss_tc04}


TC04 is the only scenario in which both controllers record 0\% success, yet the
causal mechanisms are entirely distinct. The classical controller fails because
the fixed supervisory-regulatory interface cannot respond to dynamic obstacles
faster than the A* replanning cycle permits. The 10\,Hz replanning rate and the
ICP keyframe creation threshold of approximately one keyframe per 0.8\,m of travel
introduce a latency window during which the prior waypoint sequence remains active.
This latency is not a consequence of poor tuning but of the fixed information
pathway: the low-level PID has no access to sensor data and therefore cannot
initiate avoidance until a revised waypoint sequence arrives from the supervisory
layer \cite{li_hierarchical_2023, zhu_hierarchical_2024}.

The UHRC fails for the unrelated reason of pre-existing policy instability, but
the architectural analysis of its behaviour during coherent trials is informative.
In those trials where the vehicle remained on a stable path until $t = 3.0$\,s,
the LiDAR observation was updated within the same 10\,ms inference cycle as the
obstacle revelation, and the observation embedding reflected the new obstacle
geometry immediately in the next H-L reasoning cycle. This is structurally
impossible for the classical controller regardless of tuning. The UHRC-PID hybrid
architecture in which the UHRC generates attitude references consumed by the
inner PID attitude loops achieved a 20\% success rate on TC04 in supplementary
evaluation, providing direct empirical evidence that the UHRC's reactive spatial
reasoning is functionally present when the action generation instability is
decoupled from the trajectory generation task. This result motivates the hybrid
architecture as the more immediately viable path toward deployment.


\subsection{Distributional Fragility vs Integral Robustness: TC05}
\label{sec:discuss_robustness}


TC05 most directly characterises the fundamental trade-off between the two
architectures. The classical controller's 100\% success rate under simultaneous
perturbations of mass ($\pm 20\%$), inertia ($\pm 30\%$), drag ($\pm 50\%$),
and thrust coefficient ($\pm 15\%$) demonstrates that integral feedback is a
principled and highly effective mechanism for parametric robustness. The
deviations in effective plant dynamics are treated as persistent disturbances
by the PID integrators and compensated continuously, without any requirement
for the controller to have knowledge of the perturbation magnitude or structure.
This robustness is architecturally guaranteed, it follows from the stability
analysis of the cascaded PID structure under the small-signal linearisation
used for gain selection, and does not depend on the specific perturbation
values applied.

The UHRC's 13.3\% success rate under TC05 and its elevated ITAE of
73{,}011.560\,m\,s$^2$ approximately $5\times$ larger than the next worst
scenario confirm that parametric uncertainty is the most damaging
distributional shift the policy encounters. Behavioural cloning on a single
nominal dynamic model produces a policy whose implicit representation of the
plant input-output relationship is calibrated exclusively to the nominal
parameter set \cite{le_mero_survey_2022}. When the effective dynamics deviate,
the L-module's torque predictions become systematically biased, and the
recurrent carry which encodes compressed representations of past observations
and subgoals rather than explicit physical model parameters provides no
mechanism for detecting and compensating the shift. The high-frequency torque
oscillation observed in Figure~\ref{fig:tc05_torque_uncertainty} is the
behavioural signature of this mismatch. The model generates rapid corrective
torques in response to observation errors produced by the biased dynamics,
which themselves produce further observation errors in a destabilising cycle.

The comparative results across TC01--TC05 therefore establish a clear
performance-robustness boundary. The UHRC offers reactive spatial reasoning,
fixed and bounded inference cost independent of mission duration, and a unified
architecture that eliminates manual interface design. The classical controller
provides formal stability guarantees, interpretable failure modes, integral
disturbance rejection, and robustness to significant parametric variation
without retraining. The UHRC's advantages in spatial reactivity are currently
negated by policy instability attributable to the limitations of offline
behavioural cloning from a single nominal expert. Closing this gap is a
question of training methodology rather than architecture.


\subsection{Implications for Future Development}
\label{sec:discuss_future}


Three targeted interventions follow directly from the failure analysis above.

\textbf{Domain randomisation.} Training on a distribution of dynamic models rather
than a single nominal plant is the most direct remedy for the distributional
fragility demonstrated in TC05. Randomising mass, inertia, drag, and thrust
coefficients across episodes during data generation would expose the behavioural
cloning objective to the same perturbation regime applied at evaluation, allowing
the L-module to learn torque predictions that are robust to parametric variation
\cite{block_is_2024}. This requires no architectural change and is implementable
within the existing training pipeline.

\textbf{Reinforcement learning fine-tuning.} The compounding error instability
in TC01--TC03 is a known failure mode of pure behavioural cloning that RL
fine-tuning specifically addresses \cite{almahamid_reinforcement_2021}. Initialising
from the behaviourally cloned policy and applying a shaped reward signal that
penalises trajectory divergence would allow the policy to discover corrective
strategies that the offline expert data does not contain. The UHRC's transformer
architecture is differentiable end-to-end and compatible with policy gradient
methods.

\textbf{UHRC-PID hybrid deployment.} The 20\% TC04 success rate of the
UHRC-PID hybrid architecture, compared to 0\% for both pure architectures,
is a significant result of this investigation. In the
hybrid, the UHRC generates attitude references consumed by the inner PID
loops, confining the consequences of torque prediction error to a slower
timescale where the attitude PID provides a stabilising correction. This
architecture inherits the formal stability guarantee of the inner loop while
retaining the UHRC's reactive spatial planning advantages, and represents
the most viable near-term path toward deployment.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}


This paper presented the Unified Hierarchical Reasoning Controller (UHRC), the
first systematic application of the Hierarchical Reasoning Model (HRM) architecture
to a continuous nonlinear closed-loop control system. The UHRC was implemented as a
two-level recurrent transformer in which a high-level module generates body-frame
velocity subgoals and a low-level module translates those subgoals into body wrench
commands, trained via behavioural cloning on 500 A*-guided expert episodes. A
persistent 16-slot rolling carry buffer, RoPE positional encoding, and a gated
subgoal injection mechanism were introduced as principled adaptations of the
discrete HRM architecture to the requirements of continuous real-time control.

The comparative evaluation against a classical SLAM-augmented cascaded PID baseline
across five structured test cases produced four principal findings.

First, the HRM architecture can learn a functional continuous control policy. The
UHRC successfully navigates to the goal in short-range trials across all scenarios,
and its approach trajectories in constrained environments (TC02, TC03) demonstrate
geometrically appropriate obstacle-aware subgoal generation. This constitutes the
first empirical demonstration of HRM-based closed-loop control of a nonlinear
dynamic system.

Second, policy instability attributable to behavioural cloning's compounding error
limitation is the primary obstacle to reliable deployment. The UHRC's mean success
rate across TC01--TC03 is 4.4\%, with trajectory divergence rather than collision
as the dominant failure mode in over 90\% of trials. This is a training
methodology limitation, not an architectural one.

Third, the two architectures fail in qualitatively distinct ways that reveal
complementary design boundaries. The classical controller's 0\% success rate on
TC04 is caused by a structurally irreducible replanning latency at the fixed
supervisory-regulatory interface. The UHRC's 0\% rate on the same scenario is
caused by pre-existing policy instability that prevents the vehicle from remaining
stable long enough to encounter the dynamic obstacle. The UHRC-PID hybrid achieved
20\% success on TC04, providing direct evidence that the UHRC's reactive spatial
reasoning is functionally present when action generation instability is externally
stabilised.

Fourth, the performance-robustness trade-off between unified learned hierarchy and
classical explicit decomposition is architectural in origin. The classical
controller's integral feedback provides guaranteed parametric robustness without
retraining; the UHRC's behavioural cloning objective calibrates the policy
exclusively to the nominal plant, making it fragile to distributional shift.
The 13.3\% success rate and ITAE of 73{,}011.560\,m\,s$^2$ under TC05
parametric perturbations, compared to 100\% success and ITAE of 144.978\,m\,s$^2$
for the classical controller, quantify this boundary precisely.

The primary direction for future work is domain randomisation during training,
which directly addresses distributional fragility without architectural modification.
Reinforcement learning fine-tuning from the behaviourally cloned initialisation
would address compounding error instability by allowing the policy to discover
corrective strategies absent from the offline expert. The UHRC-PID hybrid
architecture, which decouples spatial reasoning from action generation stabilisation,
represents the most viable near-term path toward operational deployment and warrants
systematic evaluation across the full test case suite. Beyond these immediate
extensions, the UHRC framework provides a principled foundation for investigating
whether unified hierarchical reasoning can match or exceed the performance of
explicit architectural decomposition once the training methodology limitations are
resolved a question this investigation has framed precisely but not yet answered.
